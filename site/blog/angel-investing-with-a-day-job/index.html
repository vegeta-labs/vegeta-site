Angel investing with a day job: three questions to clear first

Your job is why founders want you and also where your conflicts come from, so sort out what you know, what you control and what you have promised before you invest.

Updated 7 November 2026

Piccolo runs procurement at a logistics company. Bulma offers him a place in her SPV into Senzu Labs, a startup selling route-planning software. His experience is the reason she called: he knows how buyers in that market behave. Senzu is also in early talks to sell to his employer.

Nothing here stops him investing. It does mean three questions need an answer before he signs, and they apply to almost anyone who invests alongside a working life.

What do I know because of my job?

There is a difference between knowing how a market works and knowing a specific fact your employer has entrusted to you. Years in procurement teach you how long enterprise contracts take. That belongs to you. The budget for next year's route-planning tender does not.

Test each piece of your thinking by asking where you learned it, and whether you could say so aloud to your employer. If the case for the investment rests on something you could only know from inside, stop until that has been dealt with. The usual sources of duty are your employment contract and confidentiality terms, which also tend to survive after you leave.

Securities law may apply too, and the test depends on where you are. The SEC describes illegal insider trading as buying or selling a security, in breach of a duty or relationship of trust and confidence, on the basis of material non-public information. Tipping someone else can also be a violation. In the UK, the Market Abuse Regulation applies to instruments on regulated markets and trading facilities, and to certain related instruments. Shares in a private startup will usually be outside it. But if your employer's plans would move the price of a listed supplier or customer, a personal trade in that company is a different matter altogether.

What can I influence?

Piccolo's second problem is more obvious. If he helps decide whether his employer buys from Senzu while holding a stake in Senzu, his judgement is compromised, and it will look that way even if he acts fairly. Disclosure is the minimum. Removing himself from that decision may be what his employer requires, and the policy on outside interests should say.

Do this before committing rather than after. A disclosure made once the money has moved looks like a confession, and it leaves your employer with no real choice. If the answer is no, the cost of declining a deal is small next to the cost of an undisclosed interest in a supplier.

Pressure can run in subtler directions. A warm introduction from someone in your position can feel like an instruction to a junior colleague. Tell the recipient you hold a stake, and make it easy for them to say no. Never suggest that your employer stands behind a company you have backed.

If you are a company director, the duty is more formal. For UK companies, the Companies Act 2006 requires a director to avoid situations where an interest conflicts, or may conflict, with the company's, and this extends to exploiting information or opportunities. A director with an interest in a proposed transaction with the company must declare its nature and extent to the other directors. This can bite in both places: the board you sit on at work, and any startup board you join.

What have I promised?

Founders hear "happy to help" as a commitment, and it is easy to say over coffee. Replace it with something you can deliver: one review of a sales contract template this quarter, or a single named introduction, with permission. Then keep to that.

Look hard at any arrangement where you receive equity for advice. Investing your own money is usually a simple matter of disclosure. Advising looks more like outside work, which many employment contracts restrict. It also raises the question of who owns what you produce, because contracts commonly assign work done in your field to your employer. Get the answer in writing before the first session.

Then be realistic about time. Backing a company takes a morning; owning it can take a few hours a month when something goes wrong, and things go wrong during your busiest quarter. Cap the number of holdings you will actively help. A smaller group you serve properly does more good than a long list of people you avoid.

Leading a deal is a much bigger step than joining one. A lead has other investors relying on them: the assessment, the terms, the allocation, and years of updates and decisions. It also raises its own regulatory questions, which depend on where you and your investors are. Treat it as a second job, and check it against your contract as one.

A record that takes ten minutes

Keep one page, updated whenever something changes:

Company Holding and date Link to employer Told whom, when Approval What I promised
Senzu Labs £10,000 via SPV, March Prospective supplier Compliance and my manager, February Yes, recused from tender One contract review

This protects you more than any policy can. If someone asks in three years why you recused yourself or what you knew in February, the answer is written down with a date on it. Revisit it when you change employer or get promoted, since a new budget or a new board seat can turn an old holding into a new conflict, and a former employer's secrets stay secret.

Piccolo, in this version, tells compliance first. He stays out of the tender and offers Bulma what he can honestly give: an informed view of how buyers think, drawn from experience rather than from his employer's files.
