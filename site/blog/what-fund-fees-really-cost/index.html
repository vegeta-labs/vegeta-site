For investors

Two and twenty, followed through a fund's whole life

A fund's headline terms tell you little about what investors pay or how the manager gets paid. The fee schedule and the waterfall tell you.

Updated 7 November 2026

"Two and twenty" sounds like a complete description: a 2% management fee and 20% of the profits. In practice, each half raises questions the phrase does not answer. Following one fund from first close to final distribution shows what matters.

Two kinds of money, for two different purposes

The fee is paid whether or not the fund makes money. It normally goes to the management company and has to cover salaries, research, travel and overheads. A £30m fund charging 2% brings in £600,000 a year, which is the firm's revenue and not a partner's pay.

Carry goes to whoever holds the entitlement, often several partners and staff under vesting terms. It arrives only if there is profit and may take a decade. Managers often put their own money into the fund as well, and that investment rises or falls like anyone else's.

These streams carry different risks and arrive at different times. A new manager cannot pay next year's rent from carry that may never arise. An investor should also notice that the fee stream grows with fund size. Metrick and Yasuda's study of 238 private equity funds estimated that about two-thirds of expected manager revenue came from fixed components insensitive to performance. It is a model of older funds and not a measure of today's earnings, but it shows why the fee deserves as much attention as the carry.

The rate means little without the base

Two per cent of what, and for how long? A flat 2% on commitments for ten years costs 20% of the fund. Many funds do not stay flat. In the working-paper version of that study, most funds gave some concession after the investment period, and median lifetime fees were 17.75% of commitments for venture funds and 12% for buyout funds.

Take a £30m fund that charges 2% on commitments for four years, then 1.75% on the cost of investments still held.

Year Fee base Fee
1 to 4 £30m £600,000 a year, £2.4m in total
5 £20m £350,000
6 £18m £315,000
7 £15m £262,500
8 £10m £175,000
9 £6m £105,000
10 £3m £52,500
Total £3.66m

That is 12.2% of commitments, against 20% on the flat schedule, with the same "2%" on the cover page. Yet the fee in year 9 is about a sixth of what it was in year 1, while the remaining holdings may still need attention. An investor should ask how the manager will pay for that work, particularly if an extension is likely.

The wording that sets the base deserves close reading. Does a write-off reduce it at once? A partial sale? Does a fee continue during an extension? "Remaining investments" means nothing until the agreement defines it.

ILPA's principles, written from the investor's side, ask for a fee model during fundraising and a budget from first-time funds or those charging above-average fees. They also suggest a step-down to a percentage of unrealised cost after the investment period. These are recommendations and apply only where they have been agreed.

Costs outside the fee

The fee is rarely the only charge. Audit, administration, legal work and other expenses may be charged to the fund on top. The question is whether any function gets paid for twice. ILPA's view is that overheads and staff salaries belong under the management fee and should not be allocated to the fund, but the agreement decides.

Then there are fees paid by portfolio companies to the manager, such as for monitoring or board roles. An offset clause credits some or all of these against the management fee. ILPA's position is that such fees should be fully offset. Ask for a reconciliation showing the gross fee, each offset and the net amount charged, and what happens to any unused offset at the end of the fund.

From gross to net

Now the same fund to the end, with a whole-fund waterfall and no hurdle. Investors pay in the full £30m. Fees take £3.66m and other costs £1.34m, so £25m goes into companies. Those investments return £75m.

Line Amount
Returned by investments £75m
Multiple on invested capital 3.0x
Paid-in capital returned to investors £30m
Profit £45m
Carry at 20% £9m
Total to investors £66m
Multiple on paid-in capital 2.2x

Both multiples are correct, and they describe different things. The 3.0x tells you what the manager's picks did. The 2.2x tells you what investors got. A pitch quoting one should not be compared with a report quoting the other.

Notice too what the waterfall does here: carry is charged on profit after fees and costs have been returned to investors, so the manager bears 20% of those costs through lower carry. Under a different definition of profit, the result changes.

The same questions, smaller

An SPV has no annual cycle of fund economics, but the same logic applies. Is any fee a one-off or annual? Is it charged on the amount committed or invested? Who pays if the vehicle runs longer than planned, and where would that money come from once all the cash has gone into one company? Are the costs paid before or after the carry calculation?

An investor who can rebuild the table above from a fund's documents understands the terms. If the documents will not support that, that is the first thing to ask about.
