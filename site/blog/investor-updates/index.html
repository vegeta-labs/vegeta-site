Investor updates after closing: where each figure comes from

An update is only as good as the records behind it, so before it goes out, tie every figure in it to the record it came from.

Updated 27 November 2026

Explore

Bulma's syndicate closed its Senzu Labs investment in March. It is now November, the company has sent its half-year numbers, and she is writing to the eight investors. The update will have a dozen figures in it. Some come from the company, some from the SPV's own records, and one is her own judgement. Investors cannot tell these apart unless she tells them, and she cannot defend a figure later unless she knows where it came from.

Why the information comes through the lead

In a nominee SPV the investors are not shareholders of Senzu Labs. The nominee is. So when the Act says a company must send its annual accounts and reports to every member, the copy goes to the nominee, not to Goku. A member of an unquoted company can also demand a further copy without charge. Anything more, such as monthly or quarterly figures, comes from information rights in the investment documents, and who can rely on them depends on how those documents are written.

A company's articles can let a member nominate others to enjoy some of its rights, but only where the articles provide for it. In Bulma's deal neither the articles nor the documents give investors anything directly, so every document they see has passed through the nominee and the lead. That is why the update is the investors' main view of their investment, and why it has to be right.

A source for every figure

Keep a short table behind each update, one row per figure. It does not go to investors. It is what lets anyone check the update, including Bulma a year later.

Figure in the update Where it comes from Who keeps that record
Shares held for each investor The SPV's investor schedule The lead or the administrator
Total shares held by the nominee Senzu Labs' register of members The company
Amount invested and costs paid Each investor's closing statement The lead or the administrator
Company revenue, cash, headcount The company's management report, with its date The company
Value of the holding The lead's assessment, with its date and basis The lead
Reserve for the SPV's running costs The reserve ledger, reconciled to the bank statement The lead or the administrator
Tax certificates The company, once HMRC has authorised it to issue them The company

Two rows need care. Company figures are the company's, usually unaudited, and the update should say so. The value of the holding is an opinion, and it is the one figure most likely to be challenged; state it with its date and its basis.

Check the holdings before anything else

The first figures to check are the share counts, because everything else hangs off them. The nominee holds 200,000 shares of Senzu Labs on the company's register. The SPV's investor schedule must add up to the same number.

Investor Shares
Goku 50,000
Piccolo 40,000
Krillin 30,000
Tien 25,000
Four others 55,000
Total 200,000

If the two disagree, stop. A transfer may have been recorded on one side only, or a second close may not have reached the schedule. An update that goes out with the wrong count gives an investor a figure they may use for tax or estate planning.

Percentages need a dated denominator. If Senzu Labs had 4,000,000 shares in issue when the round closed, Goku's 50,000 were 1.25% of the company. If it has since issued 1,000,000 more, his 50,000 are 1.00% of 5,000,000. Both are true on their dates. Give the date of the company's share count and where it came from, or leave the percentage out.

Tax certificates are a sequence, not a date

Investors in an EIS round usually ask one thing first: when will the certificate arrive. Bulma does not issue it, and neither does the nominee. The company first submits a compliance statement to HMRC, and it can only do that once it has carried on the qualifying business activity for 4 months. HMRC then sends the company a letter of authorisation, a reference number and the compliance certificates, form EIS3, for the company to give to investors, who need them to claim relief.

The compliance statement must be submitted within 2 years of the date the 4 months are reached, or within 2 years of the end of the tax year in which the shares were issued, whichever is later. For shares issued on 10 March 2026 by a company that had been trading for years, that is 5 April 2028. So the update should say which step the company has reached, not promise a month. "The company submitted its compliance statement on 14 October and is waiting for HMRC" tells an investor exactly where things stand.

An update is not an offer

Updates sometimes carry news of a new round, and it is natural to add "let me know if you want in". That sentence can turn an update into an invitation to invest, and an invitation to invest sent in the course of business is a financial promotion. An update that carries one needs a lawful route: sent or approved by an FCA-authorised firm, or sent only to people an exemption covers and meeting that exemption's conditions. The simpler course is a separate message that meets the rules on who may be sent it.

What comes back

Each update brings replies, and some of them change the records. A new address, a death in the family, a request to move shares into a pension, a question about the certificate. One of them may ask to change the bank details for a payout, which is the one to handle most slowly. Log each reply against the investor, and update the schedule only from a confirmed instruction, so that the next update starts from records that are right.
