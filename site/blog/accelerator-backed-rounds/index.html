Investing alongside an accelerator: what the badge changes

An accelerator's stake may be bigger than the headline, its effect on the company is hard to measure, and its rights may reach your allocation.

Updated 7 November 2026

An accelerator is a fixed-term programme for a batch of young companies, usually offering some mix of capital, teaching, contacts and a final investor event. Terms and quality vary widely. If you are looking at a company fresh from one, three questions matter: what does the programme own, did it make the company better, and what does its presence do to your position in the round?

The headline percentage is rarely the whole stake

The best-known programme publishes its terms, so it makes a good worked example. Y Combinator's standard deal is currently $500,000 on two instruments: $125,000 on a post-money SAFE that converts to 7% of the company, measured after all other SAFEs and convertibles convert, and $375,000 on an uncapped SAFE with a most-favoured-nation (MFN) clause. That second SAFE converts on the terms of the lowest-cap SAFE (or other more favourable terms) the company issues between roughly the start of the batch and the priced round. Y Combinator's own example: at a $15m post-money cap it converts into 2.5%.

So the second stake is not fixed. It depends on what the company does next, and a low cap issued later makes it larger.

Suppose Senzu Labs took that deal, then raised $1m from angels on a SAFE with a $10m post-money cap, within the MFN window. The $375,000 now follows the $10m cap and converts at 3.75%. Bulma's SPV is looking at a priced round selling 20% of the company: $4m of new money at $20m post-money, above the cap, with no increase to the option pool and no other instruments outstanding.

Holder Before new money After the round
Founders 79.25% 63.40%
Accelerator, fixed SAFE 7.00% 5.60%
Accelerator, MFN SAFE 3.75% 3.00%
Angels 10.00% 8.00%
New investors 20.00%

The accelerator's "7%" is 10.75% before the new money, and the founders hold about 79% rather than the 93% a quick reading might suggest. A larger pool or another SAFE would move these figures again. Other programmes use different terms.

Founder ownership matters to you because it affects how motivated the team stays through later rounds. Always ask for the pro forma cap table showing every instrument converting at this round's price, then rebuild it yourself. If you can't reconcile it to the SAFEs themselves, you don't know what your stake is yet.

Rights that reach your allocation

Y Combinator's deal also gives it the right to buy into later financings to maintain its ownership. Other investors may hold similar rights. Using the table above, keeping 10.75% after the round means holding $2.15m of stock at the $20m valuation. The existing 8.6% is worth $1.72m, so it would need to take $430,000 of the $4m. That is more than a tenth of the round spoken for before anyone else gets a figure.

Whatever the programme, find out who has participation rights, whether they will exercise them and what is left. A lead who promises investors a $1m SPV and later discovers a $700,000 allocation has an awkward week ahead.

Don't assume that the programme's rights pass to you, either. Information rights and pro rata rights are negotiated by holder, and may depend on the size of the holding. If you invest through an SPV, the SPV holds the shares and any rights. Goku and Piccolo hold their interests in the SPV and look to it for what they get.

Selection or effect?

A programme with famous alumni may be doing one of two very different things: picking winners who would have succeeded anyway, or actually improving the companies. As an outside investor, you care about the difference. A selection signal can be priced in once everyone knows about it. The effect of a programme would show in the company's actual progress.

Few studies separate the two well, because the admitted and rejected are different to begin with. Gonzalez-Uribe and Leatherbee got close with Start-Up Chile, a government-backed programme, using a regression discontinuity design. They found no evidence that basic services (cash and co-working) improved performance by themselves. Basic services combined with entrepreneurship schooling did improve it. The result comes from one programme of one type, so it can't be carried across to every accelerator. It does suggest that the question to ask is what teaching or support this company actually received, and what it did with it.

When a programme makes claims about its own record, ask for whole batches, including closed companies and those that stopped reporting. A page of logos tells you nothing about the typical participant.

For a single company, the same question is simpler: what is different from the day it started the programme? A clearer buyer, a first paid contract, a cheaper way to deploy and a key hire are all things you can verify. "Great mentors" is not.

Demo day isn't diligence

Investor events reward short, sharp stories told under time pressure, and rounds get filled by momentum. A strong room can make a real deadline feel like a reason to skip work. It isn't one.

A few checks take little time. Separate signed revenue from pilots, and pilots from expressions of interest, since a graph can blend all three. Ask what a customer has to do for the product to work when the founder isn't in the room. Ask how much of the round is committed in writing, on what instrument and with what conditions. Then compare the price with what the same evidence would fetch without the badge. If the only thing supporting the difference is the brand, you are paying for the selection signal, and the next investor may not.

The badge can legitimately do two things for you: bring a company to your attention and give you people to call. Your memo should show which facts came from the programme and which you checked yourself.
