Does a UK syndicate lead need FCA permission? Three separate questions

Arranging a deal, running a vehicle and sending out a deal memo each sit under different UK rules, and being clear on one tells you nothing about the others.

Updated 7 November 2026

Bulma's first SPV was three former colleagues following her into Senzu Labs, with no fee. Two years on she has a list of sixty people, sends a memo every couple of months, and charges a deal fee and carry each time. Nothing changed on any single day, which is why the UK position is easy to misjudge. It helps to ask three questions separately.

The starting point is the general prohibition in section 19 of the Financial Services and Markets Act 2000: nobody may carry on a regulated activity in the UK unless authorised or exempt. Breaching it is a criminal offence carrying up to two years in prison on indictment, though a person can defend the charge by showing they took all reasonable precautions and exercised all due diligence.

Question one: are you arranging deals as a business?

Article 25 of the Regulated Activities Order has two limbs. One covers making arrangements for another person to buy or subscribe for a particular investment. The other covers making arrangements with a view to people who take part in them buying investments. A lead who negotiates an allocation, gathers investors and runs the closing can plausibly be doing both.

That activity is only regulated if the business element is met. For activities relating to securities, the FCA says the person must be carrying on a business of engaging in those activities, a narrower test than the general "by way of business" wording, since the activity has to amount to a business in its own right.

The FCA calls the business question a matter of judgement. For the by way of business test it points to the degree of continuity, a commercial element, the scale of the activity, and its share of what the person otherwise does, and says none of these is likely to be conclusive. Expect the same kinds of fact to matter under the narrower test. There is no safe number of deals and no fee level that settles it.

Bulma's first deal and her current programme score very differently on every one of those factors. The time to get a written view from a solicitor who works in this area is while the pattern is forming, since the answer shapes how the next deal is run.

Question two: is the vehicle a collective investment scheme?

This is about the structure, not the person. Section 235 catches arrangements for property of any description where participants do not have day-to-day control of its management, and where contributions and profits are pooled, or the property is managed as a whole by or for an operator, or both.

Three things follow:

  • Calling something a bare trust, an SPV or a syndicate does not answer the question. The test looks at the arrangements.
  • Investor votes do not settle it. Participants can lack day-to-day control even if they have a right to be consulted or give directions.
  • Section 235 itself has no carve-out for single-asset vehicles. One company's shares, held for named investors who each own identified shares, with no power to buy anything else, is a very different set of facts from a blind pool, and those facts are what a lawyer will test.

The stakes are real because establishing, operating or winding up a collective investment scheme is itself a regulated activity. The alternative investment fund rules ask a related but separate question, so an answer on one is not an answer on the other.

Question three: who can you send the memo to?

Section 21 stops anyone communicating an invitation or inducement to engage in investment activity in the course of business, unless they are authorised or the content is approved by an authorised person. Breach also carries up to two years on indictment. This applies even if the lead's arranging is entirely in order.

Leads who are not authorised usually rely on exemptions in the Financial Promotion Order. Two matter most for individuals investing in private companies:

High net worth individual (article 48) Self-certified sophisticated investor (article 50A)
Who qualifies Earned at least £100,000, or held net assets of £250,000 or more, in the financial year before the statement A business angel network or syndicate member for at least six months; or, in the last two years, worked professionally in private equity or SME finance, been a director of a company with turnover of at least £1 million, or made two or more investments in an unlisted company
Evidence Signed statement in the prescribed form, within the previous 12 months Signed statement in the prescribed form, within the previous 12 months

Those thresholds moved twice in 2024, rising in January and falling back in March, and statements on the January form stopped counting after 30 January 2025. Anyone holding older statements should check which form they used.

Both exemptions come with conditions that an informal email can easily miss:

  • The sender needs reasonable grounds to believe the recipient holds a current statement.
  • The investment must be of a specified kind, such as shares or debt in an unlisted company, and the investor must not be able to lose more than they commit.
  • The communication must carry the prescribed risk warning in the prescribed format, the sender's name and contact details, and an indication of the exemption and the criteria.

So Bulma cannot send the memo first and collect statements afterwards. The FCA's view is that an enquiry merely asking whether someone holds a current certificate is not itself an invitation, which gives her a clean first step. A dated register of statements, checked before each send, does the rest.

If the answer to question one is yes

There are two routes to being on the right side of section 19.

The first is becoming an appointed representative. Under section 39, someone with a contract with an authorised firm is exempt for the business that firm has accepted responsibility for in writing, and the firm answers for what they do in it. The principal must notify the FCA at least 30 days before the appointment takes effect, assess the representative first, and review it at least every 12 months. The representative's status and its principal are visible on the Financial Services Register, which is also where investors can check it.

The lighter version does not fit. An introducer appointed representative's scope excludes arranging deals in investments, so it cannot cover the main thing a lead does.

The regime may change. HM Treasury consulted in February 2026 on requiring firms to hold an FCA permission to act as a principal, with existing principals deemed to hold it. The current rules apply until any legislation takes effect.

The second route is the lead's own authorisation. The FCA has six months to decide a complete application and twelve months for an incomplete one, so that decision belongs well ahead of the deal it is meant to support.
