Leads and GPs

Running a one-person investment firm: what to settle before the first close

“Solo GP” describes who makes the decisions, not who does the work, and a one-person firm stands or falls on how the rest gets done.

Updated 7 November 2026

"General partner" has a legal meaning: a role in a partnership, set by its agreement and the law of the place where it was formed. "Solo GP" is looser. It usually means a firm where one person makes the investment decisions, whether or not there is a partnership anywhere in the structure. That person may have staff, advisers and an administrator. Plenty of people who use the phrase are running deal-by-deal SPVs and have no fund at all.

So a label will not tell you what to set up. These five questions will, and they are easier to answer before the first close than after the third.

Which money is whose?

There are at least two businesses here, and they should not share a pocket.

The management company is your business. It employs people, pays suppliers and receives whatever fees and carry have been agreed. Each investment vehicle is separate: it has its own assets, investors, costs and records, and you should establish whether its cash sits in its own account or in a shared one with a separate ledger. Write down which costs belong to which. A flight to see a portfolio company, a legal bill on a deal that died and your accounting software can each be argued either way, so settle them in advance, in line with the documents, and tell investors.

Incorporating a company gives you no permission to do anything. It is just a company.

How many deals pay for the firm?

Take a firm costing £90,000 a year to run. If each completed deal leaves £15,000 after its own direct costs, six closes cover the year:

Closes in the year Contribution Over or under £90,000
8 £120,000 +£30,000
6 £90,000 £0
4 £60,000 −£30,000

Run your own inputs, then run them again with everything arriving late. Deal fees arrive at closing, while lawyers want paying whether or not the deal closes, so a good pipeline can still leave you short in any given month. A failed deal costs money and brings in none, which raises the number of deals needed to break even.

Leave carry out of the budget altogether. It may arrive in a decade or never, and it cannot pay anything due next month. If the plan only works with carry, you need more savings or a smaller plan.

What permission does the work need?

Answer this from what you will actually do, and where. Write a page that covers who makes decisions, who sends offers, who gets paid and for what, where you and your investors are, and which entities hold assets and cash. A lawyer can work from that page far faster than from a conversation.

In the UK, carrying on a regulated activity without being authorised or exempt is prohibited, and communicating an invitation to invest in the course of business is restricted separately. Small cheques and private invitations do not by themselves take you out of either.

In the US, the Advisers Act has exemptions from registration for advisers solely to venture capital funds, and for advisers solely to private funds managing under $150 million of private fund assets, with separate conditions for foreign advisers; those relying on them are exempt reporting advisers and file part of Form ADV. Being exempt from registration is a different status from being registered, and state rules may apply as well. Other countries have their own regimes, and investors abroad can bring their rules with them.

Who checks you?

One decision-maker can move fast, but nobody is placed to tell them they are wrong, and a mistake with money can go unnoticed. Two habits help.

For investment decisions, ask someone to argue the case against before you commit: a technical specialist on the claim you cannot assess yourself, or a peer asked only for the strongest reason to pass. Get permission before sharing confidential material, and disclose any conflict. Keep what they said, including where you overruled them. A memo tidied up afterwards teaches you nothing.

For operations, split each job into preparing, checking and approving, then fill in a name for each:

Work You Someone else
Choosing investments Final decision Challenge and specialist input
Deal documents Commercial terms Drafting, closing mechanics
Investor accounts Reviewing the output Preparing them, reconciling to the bank
Payments Approving Preparing, and verifying the recipient
Valuations Reasoned view Reviewing it against the policy

Payments deserve the most attention. The classic failure is an emailed change of bank details that goes straight from one inbox to a transfer. Any such change should be confirmed through a contact you already held, by someone other than the person releasing the money. If you truly have no second person, an outside accountant or administrator can fill that seat for the payment step.

"Outsourced" is not a name. Someone at the provider has to be named for each task, along with the information you owe them and when.

What happens if you disappear for a month?

Picture a real scenario: you are in hospital, a portfolio company needs a shareholder consent signed by Friday, and a distribution is half done. Who can act? Who knows where the files are? Which decisions simply wait?

A backup needs three things: legal authority, access to records, and to have agreed to do it. A shared password supplies only one of those, and using it may breach your bank's or a provider's terms. Check what the vehicle documents, bank mandates and service agreements actually allow, and whether any investor agreement has a key-person clause that your absence would trigger.

Write this down in one page and give it to the people named on it. Revisit it when the portfolio, your team or your family circumstances change, because a plan written at formation goes out of date quietly.

Does this have to become a fund?

No. A deal-by-deal practice can run indefinitely, and raising a fund is not a required next stage. The trade is capital certainty against flexibility. A committed fund gives you money to deploy and management fees under agreed terms, along with the duties that come with them. Deal-by-deal gives investors a choice each time, and leaves you raising each time.

Test it by working through the five questions above again for the fund you have in mind, at a realistic size. If the numbers and the names work, the decision is a real one. If they do not, a fund would make those problems larger.
