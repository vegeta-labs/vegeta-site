Leads and GPs

Budget a first fund at its first close, not its target

A first-time fund runs on the money that has actually closed, and a smaller figure changes the budget, the portfolio and the strategy.

Updated 7 November 2026

Every first-time fund deck shows a target. Most of the work of running the fund happens at a different number, the amount committed at first close, and that figure can stay small for a long time. "Emerging manager" has no single definition: allocators and data providers each draw the line differently, usually around a firm's first few funds. Whatever the label, a new manager has the same problem. Most of the plan is built for a size the fund doesn't have yet.

Run the model at the figure you can count on before you commit to anything, and show it to investors before they ask.

Why the first close comes in small

Some of the shortfall has nothing to do with you. Larger allocators often have minimum cheque sizes and limits on what share of any one fund they can be. An institution with a $10m minimum that won't exceed 10% of a fund can't back anything under $100m, however good the team. A rule like that is an operating limit, not a verdict, and it's worth knowing before you spend six months pursuing that institution. It also means early money tends to come from individuals, family offices and smaller funds, who each write less and take longer to gather.

The budget

Suppose a manager is targeting $40m, charging a 2.5% annual fee on commitments, and closes on $15m.

At target At first close
Commitments $40,000,000 $15,000,000
Annual fee income at 2.5% $1,000,000 $375,000

Then set out what the firm costs to run. Say two partners on modest pay at $300,000 in total, $90,000 for legal, compliance and insurance, and $90,000 for travel, systems and everything else:

Annual costs of the management company
Partners' pay $300,000
Legal, compliance, insurance $90,000
Travel, systems, other $90,000
Total $480,000

At $15m there's a $105,000 gap every year. At $40m there's a $520,000 surplus, which is where a first hire would come from. The gap has to be covered from somewhere: lower pay, savings, a third partner's capital, a seed investor in the management company in exchange for a share of the economics. Each has a cost, and investors will want to know which you chose, because a manager who runs out of personal runway in year three is a risk to the portfolio.

Split the costs carefully between the manager and the fund. Audit, fund administration and tax filings are often fund expenses, and the partnership agreement says which are which. If the fund pays them, they come out of investable capital. A small fund feels this more, since a fixed audit and admin bill takes a larger slice of $15m than of $40m.

Also remember what you have promised. The GP commitment is often a percentage of the fund, and it has to be paid in cash by partners who may be taking low salaries.

The portfolio

The strategy deck has to survive the same test. Assume the fee stays flat for 10 years, which simplifies things, as many funds step down later. Then keep the same 40% reserves for follow-ons.

At target At first close
Fees over 10 years $10,000,000 $3,750,000
Available to invest $30,000,000 $11,250,000
Reserved for follow-ons (40%) $12,000,000 $4,500,000
Available for first cheques $18,000,000 $6,750,000

The target strategy is 30 companies at $600,000 each, which at a $10m post-money valuation buys 6%. At first close, the manager has to choose:

  • Keep 30 companies and the cheque falls to $225,000, buying 2.25%. The fund has its diversification but a far smaller stake, and may lose the seat at the table or the pro rata rights that came with the larger cheque.
  • Keep the $600,000 cheque and the fund makes 11 investments. It keeps its ownership and becomes much more concentrated.
  • Cut reserves. That protects the first cheques, but gives up the ability to back the winners later.

None of these is simply correct. They are different funds, with different return profiles, and an investor at first close is entitled to know which one they are getting. "We will reach target" isn't an answer. Saying what happens if you don't is more convincing than any forecast.

The opposite problem deserves a mention too. Some strategies only work at a small size, because the best opportunities take small cheques. A fund raising far more than that would have to write cheques the strategy doesn't suit. Size should come from what the strategy can actually deploy, which may well be under the target.

Plan for the closes in between

Fundraising usually continues after the first close, and each later close has work attached. Investors who join later generally pay their share of what has already happened, plus any interest the agreement sets, so that they end up level with earlier investors.

Suppose an investor commits $5m at month nine. The catch-up on fees alone is $5m × 2.5% × 0.75 years, or $93,750, as if they had been in from the start. There may be a similar true-up on investments already made, with amounts returned to earlier investors. The partnership agreement sets the method, and it should be run exactly as written. Whoever does the fund's operations should know this before the first close, and should build the schedule with room for it.

Timing also affects strategy. If deals made before a later close are shared across a larger investor base, early investors see their share in those holdings shrink. Consider whether a material change in the value of those holdings needs to be reflected in the price paid by later investors, if the agreement allows.

Set a floor before you need one

Decide the smallest fund you would actually run. Write down the minimum close figure, along with the matching budget, portfolio and cheque size. If the commitments come in lower, what will you do: hold off, change the strategy or stop?

Then give that version of the plan to the people who will invest first. They are taking the most risk, and they will notice if the only model you have is for a fund that doesn't yet exist.
