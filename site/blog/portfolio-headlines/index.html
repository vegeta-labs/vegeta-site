What a portfolio headline does not tell you

A large number in a press release can be true and still say little about what any investor in any fund has received.

Updated 7 November 2026

"Our portfolio companies are now worth £4bn." "Early backer of this year's biggest listing." "£100m returned to investors this year." Announcements like these are usually true. Reading them well is a matter of working out how many steps lie between the headline and your own account.

Climbing down from the company to the fund

Take one company in such a portfolio. Senzu Labs raised money last month at a £500m valuation, and all of that £500m counts towards the headline.

The firm does not own £500m of Senzu. Across its funds it holds 6%, which on a simple proportional basis is £30m. It holds that through two funds: Fund I has 4% and Fund II has 2%. If you are an investor in Fund II, the £20m in Fund I is no part of your return, however prominently the firm's website lists the company.

Even your fund's £10m is an estimate. The round price was paid for the newest shares, which often carry rights the older shares lack, so applying it to the whole company can overstate older holdings. The stake may also be subject to transfer restrictions. And between that stake and you sit fees, expenses and carried interest.

Each step asks something different:

Headline says What you still need to know
Company valuation How much the fund owns today, after dilution, and in which share class
Holding value How it was valued, and when
Fund net asset value Whether it is stated after accrued carry
Money returned by the firm How much of it came from your fund, and how much of that was cash

Growth in an aggregate is not a return

A portfolio that grows from £1bn to £4bn in combined value might have done well, or it might simply have grown. Companies added since the first figure increase the total, whatever they cost. A new round adds its own cash to the company's post-money valuation. A firm can also lose ownership to dilution through all of this, and the headline will not show it.

A return needs a fixed pool of money and a start date. The aggregate has neither.

Firm-wide cash figures have a similar problem. If a firm with three funds announces £100m of distributions, dividing that by the size of one fund produces a figure that describes nothing. The top of the fraction covers three funds and the bottom covers one. The same goes for any list of winners: a company on the firm's page may sit in an early fund, a later fund and a co-investment vehicle, each at a different price.

The three ratios that describe a fund

A fund's own figures are measured against paid-in capital: DPI for what has come back, RVPI for what is still held at reported value, and TVPI for the two together. ILPA's quarterly reporting standards list them as key valuation metrics in a fund's report.

Take a fund with £40m paid in, £12m distributed so far and £58m of remaining net asset value. Its DPI is 0.30x, its RVPI 1.45x and its TVPI 1.75x. None of the three accounts for time, so read them alongside a dated cash flow record and an IRR.

Distributions are not always cash

Funds can hand out shares instead of selling them, usually after a portfolio company lists. The fund records a distribution at the shares' value on the day. What investors then receive depends on when they sell.

Say the £12m in the example was all shares in a newly listed company, and Piccolo holds 5% of the fund. He paid in £2m and is credited with £600,000 of distributions, so his DPI reads 0.30x. If he can only sell later, after the price has fallen 30%, he banks £420,000: in cash terms, 0.21x on his £2m. The report is not wrong, because the fund did transfer that value. He simply took the market risk from that point on.

Ask whether reported distributions separate cash from shares, and which price was used.

Where the rules step in

Presenting the best slice of a record is common enough that rules address it. In the United States, the SEC's Marketing Rule covers advisers that are registered, or required to be registered, with it. SEC staff guidance explains that showing one investment or a group of investments from a private fund counts as extracted performance. Gross performance in an advertisement generally has to come with net performance. For an extract, staff said in March 2025 that its own net figure can be left out if it is labelled gross and the whole fund's gross and net performance appear alongside it, with equal prominence, among other conditions. Other jurisdictions have their own rules, and much of what circulates in press coverage is not an advertisement at all.

Either way, the request to make is the same whoever is presenting. Ask for the specific vehicle on offer, or the one you hold, with these on a single reporting date: contributions, distributions split by cash and shares, remaining holdings and how they were valued, fees and carry, and how much of the result comes from the single largest position. Everything on the firm's homepage is background until it can be tied to that record.
