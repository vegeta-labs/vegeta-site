The five questions SPV investors keep asking, and how to answer them before they do

Most investor email after closing comes down to the same five questions, so build your reporting around answering them.

Updated 7 November 2026

A single-deal SPV can sit quietly for seven years. In that time an investor forgets the share price, changes address, gets a letter from their accountant and starts to wonder whether anyone is still looking after this. Their emails rarely ask for a better newsletter. They ask one of five things, and a lead who answers them in advance gets fewer anxious messages and fewer awkward ones.

"What do I actually own?"

This one arrives years later, usually when someone is doing their tax or their estate planning, and the answer should be sitting in a single document sent at closing.

Goku's closing note from Bulma says: £40,000 accepted; £39,024 invested after his share of costs; 12,195 ordinary shares in Senzu Labs at £3.20; held by the named nominee on his behalf; the signed documents are at this link; this person handles admin questions. It takes ten minutes to write and it becomes the record every later answer refers back to.

If the terms moved during the raise, say so. An investor who signed up for £50,000 and got scaled back to £40,000 should see the final number and the reason.

"What's it worth?"

Answer with three things every time: the figure, its date and its basis. "Cost £25,000; reassessed in March and still valued at cost, because nothing since then changes the estimate" is a good answer, even when the number is dull.

Keep two values apart. The price of the latest round applies to the shares sold in that round; the SPV may hold a different class, and a later round's preferences can change what its shares would actually receive. And a higher mark is not money. Nobody can spend it, and it is not an offer from anyone to buy the shares.

If you report multiples, define them and show the sum. ILPA's reporting standards use the usual names, distributions to paid-in (DPI) and total value to paid-in (TVPI): DPI is distributions divided by contributions, and TVPI adds the current value of what remains. Piccolo paid in £25,000, has received £5,000 from a partial sale, and his remaining holding is carried at £40,000. His DPI is 0.2 and his TVPI is 1.8. The first figure is cash in hand and the second is mostly an estimate, so a report that shows only the 1.8 flatters the position.

"Do I need to do anything?"

Put this at the top of every message, even when the answer is no. "No action needed" lets an investor stop reading with a clear conscience, and "reply by 14 May" gets noticed.

The actions that matter are few: a consent on a sale or financing, a follow-on decision, a change of details, or a tax form. Each needs a deadline and a statement of what happens if they do nothing.

A follow-on is the one to handle most carefully, because it is a new investment decision. Give the new security and price, the use of funds, how allocation will work, the fees, any conflict you have, and what happens to the existing holding if the vehicle sits out. Backing the first round commits nobody to the second, unless the documents say so.

"Why haven't I heard anything?"

Silence reads as bad news, and often it is simply that the company has not reported. So publish a rhythm you can keep, such as quarterly if the company's information rights support it, and when an update will be late, send a line saying what is missing and when you will write next.

A short standing format helps, since people notice change when the layout stays the same:

Senzu Labs, quarter to 30 September. No action needed. Revenue £210,000, up from £180,000. Cash £640,000, spending £80,000 a month, so about eight months at the current rate. All figures from company management, unaudited. Holding valued at cost, reassessed this quarter with no evidence of a change. Two risks to watch: the round needs to start by January, and the largest customer's renewal is still unsigned. Next update by 31 January, or sooner if either changes.

The arithmetic is on the page (£640,000 ÷ £80,000 = 8), the source of the numbers is named, and the lead's opinion is kept apart from the company's facts.

Serious events do not wait for the quarter. A financing, an insolvency risk, a sale offer or a request for consent gets its own message. State what happened, what is known, what is not, and when you will next write, without padding it with good news from elsewhere or promising a rescue no one has agreed to.

Company documents need thought before you forward them. The vehicle may have received them under confidentiality terms or information rights that limit who can see them. If you cannot share something, say so and pass on what you are able to.

Mistakes need the same directness. Name the figure that was wrong, give the corrected one, and explain the change. Quietly swapping the PDF leaves investors holding different versions, and it will be noticed eventually.

"When do I get my money, and what do I tell my accountant?"

The honest answer to the first half is usually that nobody knows. Say that, along with anything concrete, such as a sale process under way, and avoid forecasts you cannot back.

When money does arrive, the statement should let an investor rebuild their own number: gross proceeds, costs, carry, the amount allocated to them, and whether anything is held back or could still come. Say whether they are receiving cash or securities.

For tax, list only what the vehicle actually produces and when. If a document depends on someone else, name the dependency and the next date you will update them. Accountants ask precise questions, so a precise "we do not produce that form" is more useful than a vague promise.

Keep the answers somewhere they can be found. Investors ask the same things in different words, and the second person to answer should give the same answer as the first.
