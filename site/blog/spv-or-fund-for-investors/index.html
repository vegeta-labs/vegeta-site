SPV or fund: what the same £100,000 does in each

A side-by-side look at how one investor's money moves through a single-deal vehicle and through a fund, from signature to payout.

Updated 7 November 2026

Piccolo has £100,000 to put into startups. Bulma offers him two ways to do it. One is a vehicle buying shares in Senzu Labs next month. The other is a fund she is raising to back about 25 companies she has not yet chosen. Comparing what happens to the money over time shows the real differences better than comparing the labels.

On the day he signs

In the SPV, Piccolo can read the company's numbers, the price and the rights attached to the shares before he decides. When he signs, the question he is answering is "this company, at this price?"

In the fund, there is no company to read. He reviews a strategy, a team, a track record, and a set of limits on what the fund may buy. His question is "this person, within these limits, for about a decade?" Everything that follows comes from that difference.

When the cash leaves

The SPV usually takes all his money at once, within days or weeks of closing, and pays it out in one go. After that he owes nothing further unless the documents say otherwise.

The fund takes it in stages. His £100,000 is a commitment, and Bulma issues capital calls over several years as she finds investments and incurs fees. In the first year perhaps £20,000 is called, leaving £80,000 he must still be able to produce at short notice. Fund agreements usually impose serious consequences on an investor who cannot pay when called, so that undrawn £80,000 is a real liability even though it sits in his own account.

How much gets invested

Take a 3% one-off fee on the SPV, paid out of his £100,000. That puts £97,000 into Senzu Labs.

Now take a 2% annual management fee on commitments in the fund, charged at the same rate for ten years. That costs £2,000 a year, or £20,000 in total, which leaves £80,000 to invest. Spread equally over 25 companies, that is £3,200 in each.

The comparison holds whatever the levels: fund fees recur and SPV fees mostly do not. An SPV has only one company to research and hold, and a fund pays for a manager to keep looking.

What can go wrong

The SPV's outcome rests on one company. If Senzu Labs fails, Piccolo loses the lot, and that vehicle holds nothing else to make up for it.

The fund can absorb failures, as long as enough other investments do well. Its risk is different: he has handed the selection to Bulma, and he cannot pull out of the deals he would have turned down. If her judgement is poor, he finds out slowly and with little recourse.

Who makes the later decisions

In either route, someone will face decisions years after the first signature. Should the holding take part in the next round? Should it accept a lower-priced offer? Can a shareholder sell a portion early?

A fund's manager generally decides within the mandate, and investors have whatever consent rights the agreement reserves. An SPV's answer depends on its documents. They might give the lead wide authority, require an investor vote, or leave a gap nobody noticed until the offer arrived. Investors tend to assume that a single-deal vehicle means they stay in control, and that is often untrue.

Follow-ons show the gap most clearly. A fund usually keeps reserves for its best companies. An SPV has usually spent all its money on day one, so taking up a pro-rata right means raising fresh money from whichever investors want in. Those who decline get diluted relative to those who accept.

When to get out

Neither route offers a reliable exit before the company or the fund reaches its own end. A fund's stated term can be extended under its agreement. An SPV lasts until the company is sold, lists or fails, which can take much longer than anyone guessed on signing day. In both cases, selling your interest early means finding a buyer and getting any consents required.

How to choose between them

The choice usually comes down to two honest questions.

Can you judge this company yourself, or at least judge why this lead has picked it? An SPV asks you to answer that every time. If you have neither the time nor the context, a fund's delegation is what you are paying for.

Can you live with the concentration or the commitment? An SPV is one concentrated bet, and you need several to build a spread. A fund gives you the spread, in exchange for a long commitment and a fee on money that may not yet be working.

The two can sit together. An investor can hold a fund for breadth and take selected SPVs where they have a view, as long as they read each document as its own contract and do not assume the terms match.
