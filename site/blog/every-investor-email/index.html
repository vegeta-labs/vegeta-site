Trunks

Every investor email, ready for a person

Much of the work of running an SPV or syndicate still happens by hand, in a shared mailbox. What Trunks prepares for each kind of email, and what a person still decides.

Updated 9 November 2026

One mailbox, every kind of request

Investors ask whether their payment has arrived. They send passports, bank confirmations and signed documents. They want a tax certificate or a letter for their bank. They want to withdraw, or they cannot log in. Companies and lawyers write in too, and so does anyone pretending to be them.

All of it lands in one or two shared mailboxes. Every email needs the same work before anyone can answer it: read it, work out what it is, find the investor and the deal, check the ledger, decide who should handle it and write back. None of that is hard. It is repeated for every email, and when the team is busy, questions wait.

The emails that can do the most damage look like all the others.

What Trunks does with every email

Trunks connects to the mailbox read-only. A model classifies the request and extracts fields. Code reads the amounts, references and UETRs itself. Trunks looks for the contact, the commitment and the deal, and says when there is none. Fixed rules set the risk, and the model can never lower it. Code, not the model, chooses the reply template. The case goes into the queue for that kind of email, with a due time.

So a person opens a case with the sender, the commitment, the deal, the ledger state and a draft reply already on it. They check it, approve it, copy the text and send it from their own mail client. Trunks never sends email.

What it prepares

This is the usual result. When the risk is high, the only reply available is a fixed acknowledgement.

A newsletter stays unclassified, with no reply prepared. Every category is on the Trunks page.

Three emails show it in more detail, from routine to dangerous.

“Did my payment arrive?”

Goku asks whether the payment for Senzu Labs has arrived. The question is short. By hand, someone works out who is asking, finds the commitment, opens the ledger and writes back, then does it again for the next investor.

Trunks does the lookup and code picks the reply from your records:

  • Paid in full: “We have received GBP 10,000.00 for Senzu Labs (reference SEN-001). Your commitment of GBP 10,000.00 is paid in full.”
  • Not in full, and a payment date under five working days old: a reply saying bank transfers can take a few working days to arrive.
  • Not in full, with an older date or none: a reply asking for the payment confirmation, unless a valid one is already attached.
  • No commitment found: a reply asking for the deal name and the payment reference.

The person gets the answer and the ledger beside each other, and checks one against the other instead of looking it up.

An MT103 for a payment you cannot see

Yamcha paid from a bank abroad. The money is not in your ledger, so Yamcha sends the bank’s MT103. By hand, someone opens the file, finds the investor and the deal, compares the amount and works out what to say. An MT103 shows what the bank was told to send, not what arrived, so replying “received” on the strength of it would be wrong.

From a text MT103 or a pacs.008 file, Trunks reads the UETR, the amount and the value date, and links the commitment. If the email states one amount and the file shows another, the case goes to high risk. Only your imported ledger can show a payment as received, so nothing in the email can make the reply say the money has arrived.

The account numbers in the file normally count as a change of bank details. Trunks sets that aside only when it can check them: a valid MT103 text file or pacs.008 file whose beneficiary account is on your list for that deal, and the other conditions here. Anything else takes the cautious route below, including a bank’s printed PDF, so some genuine confirmations do too. A valid message whose beneficiary account is not on the list goes to high risk.

The person gets a case showing who paid, for which deal and how much, with the UETR ready if the bank needs to trace it. They check the bank statement. Once the payment is in the ledger and imported, “Relink with the latest directory” updates the case and the reply.

A new bank account

An email asks you to pay a new bank account, from an address that only looks right. It reads like any other email, and by hand the only defence is someone noticing.

Once Trunks recognises a change of bank details, by code or by the model, fixed rules take over. The risk is set from the category and from signals such as “Look-alike domain”. The only reply available is a fixed acknowledgement, and Trunks creates a verification task:

  1. A person calls the contact on the number already on file, notifies the old contact with a notice Trunks prepares, and completes a checklist.
  2. A cooling-off period runs, 24 hours by default.
  3. A different person, with the approver role, approves.

The steps are on the case, and what was done is recorded.

What your team gets

  • Each email it processes becomes a case in a queue with a due time, before anyone has opened it.
  • The lookup is done first: sender, deal, commitment and ledger state are on the case.
  • Replies start from your own fixed text and your records. A reply that shows record data goes only to a sender who is authenticated and entitled to see that commitment.
  • Likely ID documents go to your KYC folder or webhook, with a daily reminder while a transfer has failed.
  • Risky emails follow a fixed path, with a record of who did what.

What a person still does

Someone imports the deals, commitments, bank lines, ledger and contacts as CSV files. Commitments and the ledger exported by Goten import directly; bank lines and contacts are your own files. Someone checks every reply, approves it, sends it and marks it as sent.

Trunks does not replace judgement. It has no connection to your bank and cannot stop a payment. An approver or admin can still close a case with its task open, giving a reason. It does not read images or scans, so a request that appears only in one is not recognised. Authenticated means the message passed DMARC and the sender is in your directory; a taken-over mailbox would pass, which is one reason a person checks every reply.

Where it runs

Trunks runs on your own server. Before text goes to a model, it masks recognised sensitive values; amounts, references and unrecognised personal information can remain. If you run a local model through Ollama inside your network, the model requests stay there.

How it was tested

One run with one model, Claude Opus 5.5, on 126 fictional emails that development never read, measured on v0.3.0. An earlier set of the same kind failed and led to two fixes in that release. Malicious bank changes and look-alike senders were rated high in 18 of 18. The contact, commitment and deal were linked correctly in 123 of 125. The test emails that passed the payment message checks were all pacs.008; MT103 was covered only by development tests. The release notes have the full table, and a scoring error that was fixed and re-scored.
