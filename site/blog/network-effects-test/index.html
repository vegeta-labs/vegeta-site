How to test a network-effects claim in a pitch deck

If half the users vanished tomorrow, would the rest be worse off? That question separates a network effect from ordinary growth.

Updated 7 November 2026

Most decks that claim a network effect are showing a growth chart. Growth can be bought. A network effect is narrower. Katz and Shapiro's 1985 article, an early treatment of the idea, studied goods whose value to one user rises with the number of other users. That is a claim about value, and it can be tested.

The removal test

Imagine half of the company's users disappear overnight, chosen at random. Would the remaining users notice?

For a messaging tool used by a team, they notice immediately. For a marketplace, buyers find fewer sellers and wait longer. For a good accounting product that has 10,000 customers, nothing changes: the software works just as well, even though the company's revenue halves. That company may be excellent. It just doesn't have a network effect, and its growth doesn't make it harder to compete with for that reason.

Three things are commonly passed off as network effects, and all fail the removal test:

  • Virality is how users arrive. A product can spread by referral without being any more useful once they have.
  • Economies of scale lower the supplier's unit cost. Users may benefit through price, but not because other users are present.
  • Switching costs keep customers in place because leaving is painful. They can be valuable, and they have nothing to do with how many other customers there are.

Which kind is being claimed

Pin down the mechanism, because each has a different weak point.

With a same-side effect, users benefit from more users like themselves, as with colleagues on a shared tool. The weak point is relevance: usually only the people you deal with count. A tool adopted by a million strangers does little for a single team, which limits how much the effect helps the company win its next customer.

With a cross-side effect, one group benefits from another: buyers and sellers, riders and drivers. Each direction has its own strength, and they can pull apart, since extra sellers help buyers but compete with the sellers already there. Rochet and Tirole's work on two-sided markets showed how much depends on how a platform divides its charges between the two sides. So when a marketplace charges one side very little, that is a decision to examine, and may be more than a temporary promotion.

With complements, outside developers build things that attract users, whose presence attracts more developers. The weak point is padding. A directory of 300 integrations proves little if five are used. Ask which ones customers actually rely on, and who maintains them.

Data improvement is the fourth common claim: each customer's data makes the product better for the others. It is easy to oversell: the benefit can flatten, the extra data may be repetitive and the improvement may have come from engineering. Ask whether the company is even permitted to reuse customer data across accounts, and ask for performance measured by the amount of data.

A business can have several of these at once. Test each on its own, so that proof of one doesn't get counted for all.

Find the network that actually matters

The company's total user count is usually the wrong number. The effect works inside a boundary: a city, a trade, a team. A spare-parts marketplace for boatyards doesn't get better for a yard in Cornwall because sellers joined on the Clyde, unless parts ship between them cheaply. Find the smallest unit in which people actually transact, and look at results there.

A company that spreads across 40 thin markets can post a splendid total without being strong in a single one. Investors should want the opposite pattern: a few markets where the effect is plainly visible, and a reason to think the next ones will follow.

What to ask for

Suppose that marketplace gives you this comparison:

Measure, per region Few sellers Many sellers
Active sellers of the relevant part 15 50
Median days to fill an order 9 3
Buyers ordering again within six months 30% 52%
Discounts paid per order £2 £7

The first three rows look like the real thing. The fourth is why you should keep going. The busier regions receive over three times as much discount per order, which could explain some of the repeat buying on its own. Those regions might also have larger yards, or have been open longer.

So ask for older and newer regions compared at the same age, and for what happens to speed and repeat orders when a discount ends. If the company has ever launched region by region, or switched a discount off in some places, that may be as near as you get to a controlled test. Subsidy isn't a flaw in itself: it may be the only way to start a thin market. The issue is whether the network still works once the payments stop.

Then check for three things that can quietly drain the value.

Concentration: if three sellers fill most orders, the network is three relationships. Losing one matters more than gaining a hundred inactive accounts.

Multi-homing: a seller listed on four competing marketplaces gains from each without being tied to any. The network may be real and still give this company no pricing power.

Negative effects: spam, poor listings, congestion and sellers undercutting each other can all grow with the network. Network effects also work in reverse, as departures make the service less useful and prompt others to leave.

What to write down

If Bulma were leading an SPV into this business, a line in her memo reading "strong network effects" would tell Goku and Piccolo nothing. This would tell them something:

Cross-side effect on the buyer side, within a region, once it passes about 40 active sellers. Order speed holds where discounts have ended, in two regions. Seller side unclear: most sellers also list elsewhere. Not yet shown in the three newest regions.

That states where the effect operates, how good the evidence is and what would prove it wrong. It also gives her something specific to check at the next round.
