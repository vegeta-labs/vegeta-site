Professional, high net worth, sophisticated: three UK labels that do different jobs

Being able to receive a private deal memo does not make someone a professional client, and signing to become one gives up protections worth reading about first.

Updated 7 November 2026

Investors in UK private deals get handed forms with similar-sounding words on them, and they tend to assume one signature covers everything. It does not. The labels answer different questions, under different rules.

  • High net worth and self-certified sophisticated are about whether someone can be sent a promotion. They come from the Financial Promotion Order, and the investor certifies themselves by signing a prescribed statement.
  • Retail and professional are about how an FCA-regulated firm must treat the client it is doing business with. They come from chapter 3 of the FCA's Conduct of Business Sourcebook, and the firm makes the decision.

Piccolo earned £120,000 last year and signed a high net worth statement. That lets an unauthorised lead send him a private-company memo under the exemption. If he then becomes a client of a regulated firm, he starts out as a retail client, which is anyone who is not a professional client or eligible counterparty. His statement changes nothing about that.

Two ways to be professional

Some clients are professional because of what they are. These per se professional clients include banks, investment firms, insurers, pension funds and their managers, funds and their management companies, governments and central banks, and other institutional investors whose main activity is investing.

Large companies can qualify too, though the test depends on the business. For MiFID or equivalent third-country business, a company must meet two of three size tests: a balance sheet of EUR 20 million, net turnover of EUR 40 million, or own funds of EUR 2 million. Other business has its own thresholds, such as called-up share capital or net assets of at least £5 million.

A private individual investing their own money is not on that list. They can only be professional by asking to be treated as one, as an elective professional client.

How an individual opts up

The rule in COBS 3.5.3 has up to three parts.

First comes the qualitative test. The firm has to assess the client's expertise, experience and knowledge, and be reasonably assured that they can make their own investment decisions and understand the risks, given the transactions in view. This applies in every case. It is the firm's judgement and not a box the client ticks. The FCA also says an elective professional should not be presumed to have market knowledge and experience comparable to a per se professional.

Next is the quantitative test. For MiFID or equivalent third-country business only, the client must also meet two of these three:

  • Transactions of significant size on the relevant market, averaging 10 a quarter over the previous four quarters.
  • A financial instrument portfolio, including cash deposits, above EUR 500,000.
  • At least one year working in the financial sector, in a professional position requiring knowledge of the transactions or services in view.

For other business this test does not apply, and the qualitative assessment does the work alone. Which regime a given deal falls into is a question for the firm.

Last comes the procedure. The client states in writing that they want to be treated as professional, whether generally or for a particular service or product. The firm gives a clear written warning about the protections and compensation rights they may lose. The client then states in writing, in a document separate from the contract, that they are aware of the consequences.

Goku spent two years in a professional dealing role at a bank and holds EUR 600,000 of investments and cash. That meets two of the three criteria. The firm still has to run the qualitative test against the kind of investment he wants to make and take all reasonable steps to confirm he qualifies. Two years spent trading listed bonds says little about understanding a seed-stage SPV, and a careful firm will notice that.

What changes after opting up

The firm's duties shift in specific ways. When giving advice, it can assume a professional client has the necessary experience and knowledge for the products and services they are classified for. On products where an appropriateness check applies, it may make the same assumption. So a check that might have stopped or warned a retail client may not happen.

The written warning is the document to read closely, since it has to set out clearly the protections and investor compensation rights the investor may lose. It deserves more attention than the signature page that follows it.

The status can also be limited. A client can opt up for one type of product and stay retail for everything else, which is often the more sensible request.

It can lapse as well. Professional clients are responsible for telling the firm about any change that could affect their categorisation. If the firm learns that an elective client no longer meets the conditions, it must take appropriate action, and must tell the client if that means moving them back to retail. Anyone whose qualification rests on a portfolio figure should treat a sharp fall in that portfolio as something to report.

The tests may change

In December 2025 the FCA consulted on replacing the quantitative test. Its proposals remove the two-of-three criteria, strengthen the qualitative assessment, and add a separate route for individuals with investable assets of at least £10 million, subject to informed consent. The consultation closed in February 2026 and the FCA said it would respond in a policy statement. The COBS text as updated in September 2026 still sets out the tests above.

A firm's onboarding forms should follow whichever text is in force on the day, so an investor categorised under the current tests may be asked to go through the process again once new rules arrive.

All of this is UK. Other countries have their own categories, and a status granted by a UK firm does not carry over to them.
