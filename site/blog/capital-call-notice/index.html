Reading a capital call notice, line by line

A capital call notice should let the investor rebuild the amount asked for from their own commitment, and every line on it is there so they can check it before they pay.

Updated 27 November 2026

Explore

Bulma has raised her first fund: £5,000,000 of commitments, invested over several years. Piccolo committed £200,000, which is 4% of the fund. He has paid two calls so far. A third notice has arrived asking for £28,000 by the end of the month. Before he pays, he should be able to tell from the notice alone why the figure is £28,000 and not something else.

Why a fund sends notices at all

A fund does not take the whole commitment on day one. Investors promise an amount, and the manager draws it in instalments as investments and costs come up. Each draw is a capital call, and the notice is the document that asks for it. The fund's agreement sets the rules: how much notice investors get, what a notice must contain, and what happens if someone pays late.

A deal-by-deal SPV usually works differently. Investors pay once, before closing, and the payment instructions they get then are, in effect, the only call. The lines below still apply to those instructions, but most of them matter only when there is a commitment that has not yet been drawn.

The notice, line by line

Who and which. The fund, the investor, the notice number and its date. A numbered sequence lets the investor see at once if a notice is missing.

Amount due and due date. The single figure to pay and the day it must arrive. Check that the date gives at least the notice period the agreement promises.

What the money is for. This is the line that lets an investor check the figure. Piccolo's notice splits it three ways.

Purpose Whole fund Piccolo, 4%
Investment in Senzu Labs £600,000 £24,000
Management fee, half-year £50,000 £2,000
Fund expenses £50,000 £2,000
Total £700,000 £28,000

The fee line can be checked independently. If the agreement charges 2% a year on commitments, Piccolo's fee is £200,000 × 2% = £4,000 a year, or £2,000 for a half-year. The fund-level figure follows the same way: £5,000,000 × 2% ÷ 2 = £50,000. If the notice showed £2,500, the question to ask is what base or period it was worked out on. The expenses line is harder to check from the notice, so the agreement's list of what the fund may charge as expenses is worth having open.

Commitment summary. What Piccolo has promised, what has been drawn and what is left.

Amount
Commitment £200,000
Called before this notice £60,000
This notice £28,000
Called to date £88,000
Undrawn after this notice £112,000

The two calls before this one took £60,000, which is 30% of his commitment, so he should expect the fund to have called £1,500,000, 30% of its commitments, across everyone, if every call has been pro rata and nobody joined at a later close. If some earlier distributions can be called again, the notice should say whether this call draws on them, because that changes the undrawn figure.

Payment details. The account and the reference to quote. These should match the last notice. If they have changed, treat the change as the riskiest line on the page and confirm it by calling a number you already hold, not one in the notice.

What happens if payment is late. The agreement's default clause, sometimes repeated on the notice. Under some agreements a late payer can lose part or all of their interest in the fund or be made to sell it, and the clause is worth reading before the first call rather than after a missed one.

Investors who joined later

A fund that holds more than one close often sends its later investors a first notice that is larger than everyone else's. It asks them for their share of the calls they missed, often with an extra amount that works like interest, so that early and late investors end up level. That notice should show the catch-up separately from the current call, with the rate and the period used.

The same notice from the other side

The person preparing the notices has a different check: the investors' amounts must add up to the fund total. Rounding can break that. A call of £100,000 across three equal commitments is £33,333.33 each, a penny short. Decide the rounding rule in advance, apply it the same way every time, and state on the notice where the penny went.

The second check is that each notice agrees with the record of what that investor has already paid. A notice that says "called before this notice: £60,000" when the bank shows £58,000 received means either a payment has not been matched or an investor is in arrears, and the notice should not go out until someone knows which.

The Institutional Limited Partners Association, an association of institutional fund investors, publishes a standard template for these notices. It released an updated version in 2025, meant for funds launched from 2027, or from 2026 for funds that also use its new performance template. A small fund does not need the full template, but its lines are a useful checklist of what investors expect to see.
