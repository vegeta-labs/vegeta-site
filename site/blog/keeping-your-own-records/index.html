The five records a syndicate lead should hold their own copy of

Whatever system or provider runs the deal, the questions about it arrive years later and are addressed to the lead, so the answers need to sit in files the lead holds.

Updated 27 November 2026

Explore

Five years after Senzu Labs closed, Goku emails Bulma. His accountant wants to know what his shares cost and how many he holds. By then the software, the provider and the laptop may all have changed. What she can answer depends on what she saved at the time.

Why the questions come late

An investor's tax questions follow the sale, and the sale may be many years after the purchase. HMRC's guidance on Capital Gains Tax records asks for documents showing what you paid for the asset and any additional costs, kept for at least a year after the Self Assessment deadline for the return that reports the gain, and longer if the return was late or HMRC has opened a check. So the clock on Goku's purchase records does not start until he sells. If records are lost, the same page says he must try to recreate them, and he will turn to Bulma to do it.

The vehicle has duties of its own. If it is a UK company, it must keep accounting records sufficient to show and explain the company's transactions. The Companies Act has a private company preserve them for three years from the date on which they are made, while tax law requires six years, which GOV.UK counts as 6 years from the end of the last company financial year they relate to, and names cases where it is longer. A nominee holding on bare trust is a different arrangement with different duties; ask an accountant which apply to yours.

These are minimums, and they are set for tax and company law. An investor's question or a dispute can outlast them.

The five records

Record The question it answers later
Investor list: who committed what, and what was received from each Who was in the deal, and did they pay in full?
Bank lines matched to investors Which payment on the statement was whose, and who decided?
Allocation: amount invested, fees, refund and shares for each investor What did each person's holding cost, and how many shares is it?
Each distribution calculation Why did this investor receive this amount?
Decision log Who changed what, when, and on what grounds?

The first two belong together. A list that says Goku paid £120,000 is a claim; the bank line it was matched to is the evidence. Keep the bank's own statement as well, because it is the one document in the set that you did not produce.

The allocation is the record an investor's accountant will want, and the one the nominee's split depends on. A distribution record should show every step from proceeds to each person's payment, costs and carry included, so that someone else can redo the sum.

The decision log holds the things no table shows: a receipt assigned by hand, an investor who withdrew, a fee moved from one payer to another, a scale-back rule chosen. In a dispute, the figure is rarely in question. What is asked is who decided it and when.

A format that survives

Save each record as a plain CSV file. It opens in any spreadsheet or text editor, with nothing to install or license. For an individual's tax records, the law allows the information to be preserved in any form and by any means, with exceptions, so the form of the file is not the obstacle.

Three habits make the files usable later:

  • Put the deal and the date in the file name, and never overwrite. A corrected allocation is a new file beside the old one.
  • Keep the currency in the file itself, in its own column.
  • Write the rules in a short text file in the same folder: the share price, each fee with its rate and who pays it, how rounding works, the scale-back policy, the carry terms, and the name and version of whatever software produced the figures. A table of results without its rules cannot be checked.

When to export, and where copies live

Export at events, since that is when the figures become final: when reconciliation is finished, at closing, after every distribution, and after any later correction. Export the decision log at each of those points too, and at regular intervals in between if the deal is active.

Keep two copies in two places that do not depend on each other or on the system that runs the deal, for example the firm's document storage and an encrypted drive. A second person should know where they are.

One caution applies. The investor list is personal data, and the ICO's guidance says you must not keep personal data for longer than you need it and should be able to justify the period. Write down how long you keep deal records and why, and restrict who can open the folder.

Answering Goku, five years on

Bulma opens the Senzu Labs folder. The rules file says: share price £2.00, one 2% fee paid by investors and deducted from the money they wired, no cap.

The ledger file has two lines booked to Goku: £100,000, matched by its payment reference, and £20,000 with a mistyped reference, confirmed by hand with Bulma's initials. The decision log shows that confirmation and its date.

The allocation file has one row for each investor.

Investor Received Fee (2%) Invested Shares
Goku £120,000 £2,400 £117,600 58,800
Piccolo £90,000 £1,800 £88,200 44,100
Krillin £60,000 £1,200 £58,800 29,400
Tien £30,000 £600 £29,400 14,700
Total £300,000 £6,000 £294,000 147,000

Her reply is short. Goku wired £120,000 in two payments. Of that, £117,600 bought 58,800 shares at £2.00, which is 40% of the syndicate's 147,000, and £2,400 was the fee. She attaches the allocation row, the two ledger lines and the rules file. Whether the fee counts towards his cost for tax is for his accountant to say; Bulma's job is to supply the figures and the evidence behind them.
