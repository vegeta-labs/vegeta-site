For investors

How a venture fund of funds works, from both sides of the table

A fund of funds trades an extra layer of cost for selection and access, which changes the sums for its investors and the conversation for the managers it backs.

Updated 7 November 2026

A fund of funds raises money and commits it to other funds rather than to companies. Its investor gets a share of many managers and, through them, many companies. For a manager, it is one limited partner on the register, standing in for a group of people the manager will never meet.

It sits close to a feeder but does a different job. A feeder pools money to reach one named fund. A fund of funds selects a portfolio of funds, and its investors are paying for that selection.

What the layer costs

Say an investor commits £200,000. The fund of funds charges 1% a year on commitments for ten years, and takes 5% carry on its own profit. The underlying funds, after their own fees and carry, return 2.5 times what they receive.

Step Amount
Commitment £200,000
Fees over ten years (1% a year) −£20,000
Committed onward to funds £180,000
Returned at 2.5 times £450,000
Profit over £200,000 £250,000
Carry at 5% of profit −£12,500
Paid to the investor £437,500

That is 2.19 times, against 2.5 times for someone holding the same funds directly. On these assumptions, the layer costs £62,500. Real terms vary, so run your own, including how fees change later in the fund's life and how the timing of cash flows affects the result.

What the extra cost can buy

The evidence is more favourable than the sum suggests, at least for venture. Harris, Jenkinson, Kaplan and Stucke, in the Journal of Financial Economics, found funds of funds matched or beat public market indices after fees in both buyout and venture. In buyout, they trailed direct fund investing. In venture, they performed about the same on average, which the authors read as a sign that they can identify and reach strong managers. That is an average, and funds of funds differ widely around it.

The explanation makes sense in practice. Venture outcomes differ widely between managers, and the best-known managers can be hard to reach. An investor without a team to assess dozens of managers may reasonably pay someone else to do it.

What an investor should check

Look at the portfolio first. If it holds thirty funds, each with thirty companies, some companies will appear more than once, and you may also hold them elsewhere. Ask how many underlying companies there are, how much they overlap and how much of the portfolio depends on the top few funds. Also check whether it really is committing to new funds, or also buying existing interests and co-investing, because each brings different fees and risks.

Then look at the timing. Capital reaches companies through two layers of calls, so money tends to draw more slowly, and it comes back later, because the fund of funds can only pay out after its underlying funds do. It may invest across several years, which spreads risk but stretches the wait. It also reports late by nature: it cannot finish its report until the underlying managers have delivered theirs.

What a manager should expect

If you run a fund and a fund of funds is interested, begin by finding out what kind of deal it is. A new commitment adds to your fund's total. Buying an existing limited partner's interest does not: the money goes to the seller, and your total stays the same, although you still have consent and paperwork to handle. Do not count the second kind towards your raise.

Next, find out whether you fit before you spend weeks on diligence. Does it back your stage and geography? Will it consider a first-time manager? How large a commitment can it make, and how would that compare with your fund size? It is a short conversation that can save months.

An allocator is also a professional reader of track records, so prepare yours accordingly. For every deal you cite, show the vehicle, date, amount, security and current status, and what you personally did: found it, decided it, sat on the board, or merely worked at the firm. Include the losses. Make it possible to check, within what you can legally share. A list that holds up under questioning does more than a long list that does not.

ILPA's due diligence questionnaire is a good way to rehearse the operational questions, such as governance, valuations, reporting and service providers. Its user guide says it was not designed with venture in mind, so use it as a prompt rather than a form to complete in full.

Treat an expression of interest as just that. Ask what remains between now and a signed commitment: investment committee, references, legal review, or a minimum fund size. Then plan your first close so that it works without them. An anchor that is delayed by a quarter should not stop your fund.

What it will ask for after closing

A fund of funds has its own investors to answer to, so its requests tend to continue well after it has signed. Expect questions about reporting dates, valuations and company data, and requests for side letter rights. Before you agree, check three things: confidentiality terms with your portfolio companies, who on your team will prepare each report, and what your other investors are entitled to.

Keep the costs in view. ILPA's Principles note that negotiating and complying with side letters can cost a lot, and suggest that provisions common to most of a fund's side letters should go into the partnership agreement instead. That is also a useful test for you: if a right is reasonable for this investor, should all investors have it?

The same goes for co-investment. If you offer a right, write down how it works and how allocation is decided. A vague promise made to get a close done is hard to honour fairly later, once a good deal arrives and three investors each remember the conversation differently.
