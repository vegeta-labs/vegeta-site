Leads and GPs

How to test a fund administrator or system before you sign

Sales demos show the deal that goes right, so bring the one that went wrong, then ask what would happen if you had to leave.

Updated 7 November 2026

A vehicle can outlast the person who chose its administrator, the administrator's account manager and sometimes the administrator. Nobody can predict which supplier will last. What you can test before signing is whether the work is right when the facts are awkward, and whether someone else could take it over.

Decide what you are buying

Software gives your people tools; they still do the work and answer for it. An administration service gives you people who do specified work under a contract. Some suppliers offer both, and a subscription does not imply the service.

Write one line for each recurring task, such as investor records, bank reconciliation, capital calls, distributions, reporting and tax coordination: who prepares it, who checks it, and who approves it. If a task has no name beside it after the supplier has answered, you have found the gap in the proposal. An SPV lead running single deals and a manager with several funds will fill this in very differently, and they are not really shopping for the same thing.

Bring your worst deal

Give every candidate the same small pack, anonymised, with the expected answers kept on your side. Base it on what has actually gone wrong for you. A pack for Bulma's SPV into Senzu Labs might include:

  • One investor paying from a joint account in a different name.
  • One commitment arriving as two transfers, the second one short by a bank charge.
  • A fee waived for one investor, agreed by email after the documents were signed.
  • An allocation corrected after closing.
  • A partial exit with carry.

The last item needs an answer you have worked out yourself. Say investors paid in £100,000, the exit returns £180,000, £4,000 of costs come off first, and carry is 20% of profit with no hurdle:

Step Amount
Proceeds after costs £176,000
Profit over paid-in capital £76,000
Carry at 20% £15,200
Left for investors £160,800
Goku's 40% share £64,320

If your documents say something different, for instance that costs are treated another way or there is a preferred return, change the example to match them. The point is to see whether the supplier reproduces your figure from your terms, and whether they can walk you through how they got there. A correct number nobody can explain is not much better than a wrong one.

Watch what happens at the correction. The earlier figure should still exist, along with who changed it, when and why. A system that simply overwrites it has lost the record you will want when an investor asks why two statements disagree.

Questions that sort people from slides

Ask to see each of these on screen rather than have them described:

  • Can the person who prepares a payment also release it?
  • Who changed this investor's bank details, and what checked the change?
  • Where do unmatched bank lines go, and who clears them?
  • Pick any number in a report. Can you trace it back to transactions?
  • What happens to this deal's work when the person doing it goes on leave?

Then ask who actually does the work, where they sit and who reviews them. References are most useful when they come from someone with your structure who has been through a correction, a change of staff or a completed distribution, because a reference from a client still in its first quarter has not seen any of that.

Run a handover drill

Choose one closed period and ask the supplier to assemble what a successor would need. Then give it to someone independent, such as your accountant, and ask them to follow one investor's balance from opening position to closing statement using only that pack.

Note what they could not work out. Those are the places where the explanation lives in someone's head or inbox. Fix them while the relationship is good, and agree to repeat the exercise at a sensible interval.

Look for these in the pack:

  • Transaction history and bank evidence.
  • Investor records and signed documents.
  • The calculation method, as well as its results.
  • Reconciliations, including items that were written off or explained by hand.

A pile of PDF reports does not pass. Your successor needs data they can load.

Put the exit in the contract

Negotiate this before you sign, when both sides still want the deal. The contract should cover:

  • When you can leave for persistent service failure, and on what notice.
  • What transition help the supplier owes, in named tasks, and what it costs.
  • The format and timing of the data handed over, and who owns it.
  • Who has authority to appoint and instruct a replacement.
  • Who controls the bank account, and whether that control depends on the supplier's own systems.

The last point is the one to walk through in detail. If the only people who can release funds work for the supplier, a dispute or a failure can stop a distribution. Nor should you assume a bank, custodian or software vendor will step in; each one's role extends only as far as its agreement.

Also ask how a long engagement is paid for. A vehicle may need administering for years after a single upfront fee, so ask what funds the work in year six, and how annual price rises are controlled. The answer is more useful than comparing first-year quotes, which often cover different work. A complete quote names your entities, investors, currencies and reports, plus migration, extensions and exit charges.

Check any claim of regulatory status

Regulatory status belongs to a specific entity for specific activities. In the UK, the Financial Services Register shows whether a firm is authorised or an appointed representative, and who its principal is. Check the legal entity named in the contract, since a group's branding or a subcontractor's permission does not necessarily cover the service you are buying. Outside the UK, check with the relevant local regulator.
