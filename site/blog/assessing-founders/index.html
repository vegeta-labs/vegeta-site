Leads and GPs

Assessing founders with evidence instead of charm

Investors say the team matters most, but the checks that actually reveal a team are records, predictions and specific references.

Updated 7 November 2026

Venture investors rate people above almost everything else. In the working-paper version of a survey of almost 900 institutional VCs, 95% of firms called the management team an important factor in choosing investments and 47% called it the most important. Looking back at investments that worked, 56% named the team as the main reason.

That is what investors believe, which is different from proof. Asking people to explain their own wins is a long way from measuring cause. The same survey gives a useful yardstick: the average firm spent 118 hours on due diligence per deal and called 10 references. A syndicate lead on a short deadline has far less. So the hours you do have should go where they tell you most.

What research supports

Two findings are strong enough to adjust your instincts.

Youth is not the advantage the popular image suggests. Using US Census Bureau data, Azoulay, Jones, Kim and Miranda found that the mean founder age of the fastest-growing 1 in 1,000 new ventures was 45.0, and that success rates rose with age: a 50-year-old founder was about 1.8 times as likely as a 30-year-old to reach that growth, and founders in their early 20s were the least likely. Prior experience in the same industry predicted much higher success rates too: at least three years in the broad industry went with twice the rate of reaching that top growth tier. These are base rates across many founders and do not judge any one person, so direct knowledge of the industry deserves more weight than a compelling origin story.

Past success does carry over, for mixed reasons. Gompers, Kovner, Lerner and Scharfstein found that founders with a successful track record are much more likely to succeed than first-timers or those who previously failed. They also suggest part of the effect is reputational: suppliers and customers commit more readily to someone they expect to win. So a previous exit tells you something, and may even help the next company directly, but it won't tell you how much of the first win was timing. Check what the founder did and whether it transfers.

Neither finding tells you about the person in front of you. They tell you which questions to weight.

Read the budget as a list of people

Say Bulma is leading an SPV into Senzu Labs, which has two founders and wants to raise £1.5m. Skip the biography at first and open the use of funds. Each large line implies somebody who will have to make it work.

If £500,000 is going on a sales team, who has hired and managed salespeople before? If the plan assumes the technical founder handles customer implementations as well as the product, is that one job or two? If the regulatory route takes up a year of the plan, who has been through it?

The answer can be "nobody yet, and here is the hire". That is fine as long as the hire is in the budget. A plan where two people quietly cover four jobs looks capital-efficient on paper, but it is really an unfunded cost.

Compare old predictions with what happened

This is the check with the most value for the least effort, and it is hard to fake. Ask for an investor update, board pack or forecast from a year ago. Lay last year's forecast next to this year's actuals.

Missing a forecast is normal. What you want to see is whether the misses are explained, whether the same kind of optimism repeats and whether this round's plan has learned anything from them. A founder who predicted 40 customers, reached 22 and can say exactly why, with a changed plan to show for it, has given you something a polished pitch cannot.

Then ask about a moment when something in the company had to give:

  • When did you last raise or cut a price, and what did you see afterwards?
  • Which customer did you decide wasn't worth keeping?
  • When did the plan and the bank balance last disagree, and which one won?
  • What do you believe now about your buyers that you argued against a year ago?

Follow each answer down to the document or the customer it concerns. Specifics that hold up are what you came for. Don't reward the answer that sounds most like your own career, and don't treat a founder's readiness to go unpaid as a measure of commitment. That mostly tells you who can afford to.

Check what the co-founders have actually agreed

Talk to the founders separately for part of the time. Ask each who can sign a customer contract, who can commit £20,000 of spending, and who would decide to let an employee go. Small differences are normal. If they give different answers on who can bind the company, find out how that plays out when a salesperson promises a date the engineer cannot meet.

Then check the paper, because it tells you what has been settled. Do the founders' shares vest? What happens to the shares if one leaves in month nine? Has each founder, and each early contractor, assigned their work to the company? A team that describes itself as completely aligned but has no leaver terms hasn't had the difficult conversation yet.

Make reference calls specific

Ask for someone who saw the founder do the relevant job, ideally someone who worked for them. Find out when, in what role and under what pressure. Then ask for an event: what happened when the big customer left, or the release slipped? "How would you rate them?" gets you a pleasant number.

Finding references the founder didn't suggest can be valuable, and can also damage them if word spreads that they are fundraising, or that you doubt them. Tell the founder you do this, and don't approach current employees or customers without agreement. Weigh what you hear for what the person actually witnessed, as opposed to what they heard. If something serious comes up, put it to the founder. A former colleague has their own version of events, and one negative call should send you looking for evidence rather than settle the matter.

Write it so someone can disagree

Goku and Piccolo, reading Bulma's memo, can't do anything with "exceptional founders". They can act on this:

The founders have shipped twice on forecast and lost one key account, which they explained correctly. Neither has run a sales team, and the first sales hire is in the budget at month four. Their leaver terms are unusually light for a two-person team, and that is under negotiation.

Each clause can be checked, each could change the price or the terms, and each could be proved wrong by next year.
