Write an investment thesis you could lose an argument with

A thesis earns its place when it rejects companies you would otherwise have liked, and when it can later be shown to be wrong.

Updated 7 November 2026

Take a thesis and three decks to a colleague. If the thesis cannot tell them which deck to bin, it is a slogan. "Backing bold founders in healthcare" passes every deck. This one does not:

Independent pharmacies will pay monthly for stock-control software if it connects to the wholesaler ordering systems they already use and needs no site visit to install.

Bulma, who leads SPVs into early-stage software companies, could be wrong about that sentence in several checkable ways. Pharmacies might not pay. Wholesaler connections might need a custom build each time. Installation might still take an engineer on site. That exposure is the point.

What the sentence has to carry

Four questions decide whether a thesis is doing any work.

What changed? If nothing has moved, ask why the opportunity is still open. A new data standard, a change in who holds the budget or a fall in the cost of building something are all answers. "The market is big" is not.

Who pays, out of which budget, and what do they do today instead? A problem that everybody agrees is serious may still have nobody with authority to buy a fix.

What will you refuse? Write the rules before you meet the company you love. Bulma might turn down anything needing bespoke integration per customer, or any model where the company has to hold stock itself. When an appealing founder breaks a rule, either pass or change the rule openly. A quiet exception teaches you nothing.

How does a good outcome become a good return? This is the question most often skipped, and it takes five minutes:

Step Figure
Cheque £100,000
Post-money valuation at entry £8,000,000
Stake at entry 1.25%
Three later rounds, each selling 20% Stake × 0.8 × 0.8 × 0.8 = 0.64%
Proceeds if the company sells for £100,000,000 £640,000, or 6.4 times the cheque
Sale price needed for 10 times the cheque £156,250,000

That ignores liquidation preferences, SPV costs and carry, all of which reduce the investor's share. Even so, it forces the real question: can pharmacy software plausibly be worth £156m? If the market can't support the number the thesis needs, the thesis fails on arithmetic, however good the product.

A thesis gives you no authority

A thesis is what you believe. A fund's limited partnership agreement is what you are allowed to do. Evidence can change the first overnight; the second changes only through whatever process the agreement sets out. A manager who has come to believe in a sector outside the fund's stated scope still has to deal with that restriction.

SPV leads have more freedom here, because each deal has its own documents and its own investors. The discipline runs the other way: when every deal can be described afresh, it is tempting to write the thesis after the deal arrives. A record of passes, dated before the outcome was known, is the best protection against that.

When your view is unpopular

Plenty of investors describe their thesis as contrarian. Three checks show whether that means anything.

First, is there really a consensus? Three funds declining a round may tell you about their fund size, their stage focus or their view of the price. None of that amounts to a market opinion about pharmacy software. Name the specific assumption others reject, and find out whether they reject it.

Second, why is this still available to you? Sometimes there is a good reason: the round is too small for institutions, or judging it takes knowledge few investors have. Sometimes the reason is worse: you have been shown the best three customers, or the cost of onboarding has been left out of the margin. Write down both explanations and try to prove the unflattering one first.

Third, will the deciding evidence arrive before the cash runs out? Suppose the disputed question is whether pharmacies renew. Annual contracts mean the first renewals come at month 12. If the round funds 14 months, the company has about two months of data before it needs to raise again, on a question that may need a year's more cohorts to settle. The thesis might be correct and the financing still unable to prove it.

Being alone in a view is not evidence for it. It also says nothing about how much to put in. The size of a position should follow from what you can afford to lose and how good the evidence is.

Price the follow-on as a stranger

Eighteen months later Senzu Labs, the company Bulma backed, raises again and her SPV is offered its pro rata at a much higher price. Two separate decisions tend to get blurred here.

Continuing to hold is often not a decision at all. Private shares are hard to sell, so holding says little about conviction. Putting in more is new money at a new price, and it deserves a new case: at this valuation, with this evidence and this need for further capital, would you buy if you had never owned a share?

Existing investors do know more, and that knowledge is a legitimate edge. The stranger's question tests whether the knowledge is driving the decision or whether the wish to avoid admitting a mistake is.

An SPV also has a mechanical problem. Many are built to hold one investment and have no money left, so a follow-on may need new commitments or a new vehicle, depending on the documents. Goku and Piccolo, as investors in the original SPV, will each be making their own decision, and they should get the new price and the new case rather than a reminder of the old one.

Keep it dated

Set a review date, then log what you learn from each company you see, including the ones you turn down. If renewals keep coming in below the level the return needs, the central claim is in trouble, and the log will show it.

When the view changes, save a new version and leave the old one intact. Separate two kinds of bad news. One late deployment may be an execution problem. Every deployment needing paid custom work means the sentence at the top is wrong. If every disappointment produces a new explanation that could never have been tested, the thesis has stopped being a thesis.

Before you publish it

A thesis can stay private, and publishing one can help founders find you. An essay about pharmacy software is one thing. A post that names a live round is another. In the UK, section 21 of the Financial Services and Markets Act 2000 restricts communicating an invitation or inducement to engage in investment activity in the course of business. In the US, Rule 502(c) of Regulation D bars general solicitation for offerings under the rules it covers, with Rule 506(c) among the exceptions. Check the position where you and your readers are before a thesis post starts pointing at a deal.
