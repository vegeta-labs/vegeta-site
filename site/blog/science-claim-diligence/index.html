Leads and GPs

Checking a science claim you cannot judge yourself

A syndicate lead does not need to out-think the founder on the science. The job is to find the one claim the price rests on and pay someone to try to break it.

Updated 7 November 2026

Bulma is looking at Senzu Labs, which uses engineered yeast to make a speciality ingredient. She has a business background and a term sheet. The founder has a doctorate, ten years in the field and a deck full of charts she cannot evaluate. Reading more papers will not close that gap in the three weeks she has.

She does not need to close it. She needs to know which technical statement her investors are actually paying for, and to get an honest answer on that statement from someone qualified.

Do the commercial arithmetic first

Technical diligence often starts in the wrong place, with a general expert call about whether the technology is impressive. Start with the unit economics instead, because they show which technical number matters.

Senzu's process costs £32 in feedstock and processing for each kilogram it could make at perfect yield. Customers will pay £50 a kilogram.

Yield Running cost per kg sold Margin per kg
80% (lab result) £40.00 £10.00
64% £50.00 £0.00
60% £53.33 (£3.33)

The lab result looks healthy. Lose 20 percentage points of yield in larger tanks, though, and every kilogram loses money before paying for the plant, the staff or anything else.

The vague question was "does the science work?". The useful one is now much sharper: will yield hold above 64%, and comfortably above once fixed costs are included, at the volume the business plan needs? Someone can answer that, and the answer can turn out badly. That is what makes it worth asking.

Repeat the exercise for any other assumption that could sink the plan: purity, shelf life, throughput, regulatory approval for the intended use. Most deals have two or three such claims. Write each as a sentence containing a number and a condition. If a claim cannot be written that way, the company may not yet know what it is claiming.

Buy a narrow answer

A short paid engagement with a narrow brief is worth more than a long chat with a famous name. Put the brief in writing and include:

  • The exact claim, and the data the reviewer will see.
  • What the reviewer may not have seen, so the limits of the opinion are on record.
  • Any link to the company, its investors or its competitors, including past consulting and shareholdings.
  • A request to name what else could explain the result: contamination, a measurement artefact, a favourable batch, a method that will not transfer.
  • A request to name the cheapest test that would settle the matter.

Choose someone who has run the process at the relevant scale, not only someone who has published on it. A scale-up engineer and an academic often disagree, and the disagreement is informative.

Pay for the time, and say up front that you want findings rather than a view on whether to invest. An expert asked to bless a deal tends to weigh in on the deal. An expert asked to attack a sentence tends to stay on the sentence.

Where the answer decides the investment, have the test repeated somewhere the founder's team does not control. A sample tested blind by an outside lab can cost little compared with the cheque.

Treat readiness levels as vocabulary

Founders often quote a technology readiness level. The scale is useful shorthand, but look at where it comes from. NASA's nine levels describe the road to a spacecraft: TRL 6 means a fully functional prototype or representational model, TRL 7 is demonstration in a space environment and TRL 9 is proven on a successful mission.

Other industries adapt the idea, and adaptations differ. So "we are at TRL 6" needs three follow-up questions. Which scale? Who decided? In what environment was the demonstration, and how close is it to the customer's? A level speaks to technical maturity, and a high one can still sit on top of a product nobody can sell at a profit.

Sort out the SPV practicalities early

Diligence on a syndicate deal has a practical layer that is easy to overlook.

Decide who pays. An expert fee spent before the SPV exists usually falls on the lead, and the deal budget can repay it only if the investors have been told and the deal closes. Settle what happens if the work kills the deal.

Check what can be shared. The founder's data will usually be under a confidentiality agreement signed by the lead, and it may not permit forwarding to every prospective investor. Agree a version in advance that investors are allowed to read, and ask the reviewer whether their name and findings may appear in it.

Then write down what is still open. A good deal memo says which claims were tested, by whom, against what evidence, and which were not tested at all. It should also say what the cheque buys in technical terms. For Senzu, the round might fund a pilot run that shows yield at 1,000 litres, and the next raise depends on that number. Stating the threshold before results come in makes it harder for anyone, including the lead, to redefine success afterwards.

Investors reading that memo will know they are taking science risk, and which science risk. They are entitled to that much, even when nobody in the syndicate could have run the experiment.
