Leads and GPs

Reading a venture scout agreement before your first introduction

“Scout” has no fixed legal meaning, so the agreement decides what you earn, what you may say and what you may do when the firm says no.

Updated 7 November 2026

Scouting can mean forwarding an email to a venture firm, or it can mean investing the firm's money within a set budget. Both people may call themselves scouts. What separates them is a document, and the time to read it is before the introduction that matters.

Work out the money with real numbers

The most common confusion is what a percentage is a percentage of. Suppose Bulma scouts for a firm and introduces Senzu Labs. The firm invests £250,000 and later receives £1,000,000. Profit on the deal is £750,000, and the firm takes 20% carry.

What the agreement might mean Bulma's share
5% of the profit on the deal £37,500
5% of the firm's carry on the deal (5% of £150,000) £7,500
5% of the firm's carry, but carry is calculated across the whole fund and the fund loses money £0

All three could be described in conversation as "5% carry". The third is the one people miss. If carry is measured on the whole fund, a £20m fund that returns £18m pays no carry, and a scout whose only entitlement is a share of carry receives nothing, even though Senzu Labs quadrupled the firm's money.

So read for the base. Then find out what else stands between the percentage and cash:

  • when the entitlement is earned, and whether it vests over time
  • what happens to it if you stop scouting or the firm ends the programme
  • whether you could be asked to repay money, for instance if the fund's later losses mean carry was overpaid
  • when it is paid, since a fund may take a decade to wind up

Some scouts are paid a flat fee or nothing at all. Those arrangements need the same clarity. Anything that depends on a transaction closing raises a separate question, covered below.

Decide who found the company

An entitlement is only as good as the record that attaches it to you. Firms hear about the same company from several people, and a firm may already have met a founder at a conference six months before your email.

Agree a simple rule in writing. For example: a company counts as yours if you submit it through a named process, the firm has no earlier record of it, and the firm invests within a set period. Fix what happens if the firm invests two years later, at a later round, or follows on. Then keep your side of it: dated introductions, saved replies, and a list of what you have put forward. Reconstructing that from memory after an exit is a bad position to argue from.

Be exact with founders

A founder talking to you will reasonably assume you speak for the firm. Say in the first conversation what you can and can't do: you can introduce and recommend, for example, but an investment committee decides. If you do hold a budget, say how much and what still needs sign-off.

Warm words from a scout can reshape a founder's fundraising, and they may stop talking to other investors because of them. Tell them where the decision sits. If you will be paid when the firm invests, founders may also want to know, and the agreement may say whether you can tell them.

When the firm passes and you still like it

Say the firm declines Senzu Labs and Bulma wants to put in her own money. It is tempting to think a pass frees her. It may not. Three separate parties have a say, and none can speak for the others.

The firm's agreement might give it first look at anything you find, restrict outside investments, or limit what you can do with what you learned from it. Read it, and get any permission in writing.

The founder decides who joins their round, and what they gave you was given in a particular context. Ask before you reuse the deck or the data, or pass it to anyone else. The firm cannot consent to that for them.

The law applies to whatever you are about to do, on its own facts. Writing a cheque from your own account is one thing. Bringing other people in is another.

Also, don't let the old relationship do any selling for you. If you approach anyone else, make clear that the firm looked and declined, or at least don't imply that it is behind you.

Scouting doesn't entitle you to raise money

Many scouts drift towards leading a syndicate, and the title gives no permission to do that. The legal test looks at what you actually do and how you are paid, wherever you are.

In the US, the SEC's guide to broker-dealer registration asks whether you take part in solicitation, negotiation or execution of a securities transaction, and whether your pay depends on its outcome or size. It lists finders among those who may need to register, and says a "yes" to any of its questions indicates that you may need to. A fee paid per closed deal for finding investors is exactly the kind of fact it is asking about.

In the UK, article 25 of the Regulated Activities Order makes arranging deals in investments a specified activity. Section 19 of the Financial Services and Markets Act 2000 prohibits carrying on a regulated activity in the UK unless you are authorised or exempt, and exclusions in the Order may apply depending on the facts. Other countries have their own rules, and a cross-border syndicate may have to deal with several.

Settle this before the first message to an investor, and not after a deal has started to come together. It is much easier to design compensation to fit the rules than to unwind a promise you have already made.
