Raising a fund or staying deal by deal: the sums for a lead

Fee income, carry and the long tail of vehicles work very differently in the two models. A lead should run the numbers before picking one.

Updated 7 November 2026

Bulma has led a few SPVs and is wondering whether to raise a fund. Her question sounds strategic, but much of the answer is arithmetic. Say she needs £180,000 a year to run the business: her own modest pay, one colleague, and the legal, accounting and software bills. What would each model have to deliver to cover that?

What covers the costs

Suppose she charges 3% as a one-off fee on the money raised for each SPV. To earn £180,000 she needs to close £6m every year, which might be eight deals of £750,000. There is no carry-over: on 1 January the count returns to zero, and she starts again.

Suppose instead she raises a fund and charges 2% a year on commitments. A £9m fund pays £180,000 a year, and it pays that in the years she closes nothing.

Now test each against a poor outcome. If only five of her eight deals close, she raises £3.75m and earns £112,500, a shortfall of £67,500, though the three failed deals still took up her time. If the fund closes at £6m when she was aiming for £9m, she earns £120,000 and is £60,000 short, every year, for the life of the fund.

So the risks fall differently. An SPV lead faces a recurring risk each year, and a poor year can be followed by a good one. A fund manager takes one large risk at the first close and then lives with the result.

What the fee does to the fund

A steady income comes out of investors' money. A flat 2% for ten years on a £9m fund is £1.8m, which is 20% of commitments and leaves £7.2m to invest. Some funds cut their fees after the investment period, so the real number may be lower, but investors will do this calculation and she should do it first. Her portfolio has to make its return on £7.2m and pay it back on £9m.

Carry works differently too

Suppose Bulma invests £1m in each of five companies. One returns £6m. The other four return nothing. Her carry is 20%, and fees are ignored for simplicity.

If these were five SPVs, the winner shows a profit of £5m and she earns £1m in carry. The four failures are in separate vehicles, with separate investors, and do not reduce it.

If the same five companies were in one fund that nets all gains and losses, investors got back £6m on £5m invested. The profit is £1m and her carry is £200,000.

That is a fivefold difference on the same portfolio. Deal-by-deal carry pays well on winners, and fund investors will ask why they should accept less than a portfolio-wide test. But the SPV figure relies on her filling the winning deal, and on the investors in the losing deals coming back after losing all their money. It also comes later than any fee: carry only arrives after an exit, possibly many years out, so it cannot pay the rent in the meantime.

The vehicles that do not go away

A one-off fee pays once, but the vehicle does not end at closing. Each SPV goes on needing records, investor reports, tax paperwork and responses to the next corporate action until the company is sold or fails.

If Bulma closes eight deals a year, then after five years she has 40 live vehicles if none has exited. If each costs her £2,000 a year in time and outside fees, that is £80,000 a year of work on deals that stopped paying her years ago. Check who bears this under each vehicle's documents. If nobody budgeted for it, a share of each new deal's fee is quietly paying for the old ones. Ending new deals does not end those duties.

A fund has a tail of its own, because the holdings outlast the investment period and fee income may fall as they do. But it is one set of accounts and one investor group, and those years were part of the plan from the start.

What else changes

Fundraising work moves. A fund means a long, concentrated raise followed by a period when she can focus on investing. SPVs spread the raising across every deal, indefinitely, and the same people get asked again and again.

Her freedom to act changes too. With a fund's committed capital she can make a firm offer to a founder quickly, subject to her ability to call the money and investors meeting those calls. With an SPV, her offer is only as firm as the interest she has lined up. In return, the SPV lets her back anything her investors will fund, while a fund's mandate holds her to what she promised.

And a UK manager's regulatory position changes, since a fund will generally mean operating within the alternative investment fund manager regime. That regime is under review. In July 2026 the FCA proposed a three-tier structure by firm size, with implementation envisaged in 2028, so a fund raised today could span both sets of rules. Anyone already running SPVs should also check whether those SPVs could themselves be collective investment schemes.

The test before she decides

Before choosing, Bulma should work out five figures: the smallest fund she would actually run, the fewest deals she could close in a bad year, the cost base each model needs, the annual cost of the vehicles she already has, and how long she could keep going if no carry ever arrived. If the plan only works at the target size, it does not work yet.

Running both is possible. Earlier SPVs do not vanish when a fund closes, and any future deal then has two possible homes. Investors in both will want to see, in writing, how she decides which one gets it.
