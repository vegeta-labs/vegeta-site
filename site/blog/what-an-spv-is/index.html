What an SPV is, and what it leaves you holding

An SPV is defined by its purpose rather than by a legal form, so the useful question is what your money turns into at each step.

Updated 7 November 2026

"Special purpose vehicle" tells you what a structure is for: one transaction, ring-fenced from everything else. It says nothing about what the structure is. Banks use the term for securitisation companies, landlords for property companies, and syndicate leads for whatever holds one startup investment. That last kind might be a limited company, a nominee holding shares on bare trust, or a limited partnership. Your rights, your tax position and your exit all depend on which one you have, and the letters "SPV" on a deal sheet will not tell you.

Following the money through one deal

Bulma has an allocation in Senzu Labs, which is raising £3m at a £12m pre-money valuation. That makes the company worth £15m after the round. Bulma's vehicle will buy £450,000 of new shares, which is 3% of the company.

She collects £470,000 from investors, because £20,000 goes on setting up and running the vehicle. Goku puts in £141,000, which is 30% of the money raised. Piccolo puts in £94,000, or 20%.

Goku's indirect stake is 30% of 3%, or 0.9% of Senzu Labs. At the round price that is worth £135,000, not the £141,000 he paid. The gap is his share of the costs, and it is why the amount reaching the company is the figure to check.

Senzu Labs sees one shareholder, which spares it a long register and a long list of signatures on every later document. The saving is the company's. Goku and Piccolo get something different: entry to a round where a cheque of their size might not have been accepted, in return for fees and carry.

Years later the company is sold for £50m. A later round has diluted the vehicle by a fifth, from 3% to 2.4%, so with no preference shares ranking ahead it receives £1.2m. The profit over the £470,000 invested is £730,000, and Bulma's carry at 20% takes £146,000. That leaves £1,054,000 for investors. Goku's 30% comes to £316,200.

Dilution, costs and carry are each visible in that sum. None of them shows up in a headline saying the company sold for 3.3 times its post-money valuation.

Pooling is not diversifying

An SPV may have forty investors and one asset. All forty share a single company's risk, and having more people in the vehicle does nothing to reduce it. Their money is locked up for as long as that company stays private, and there is usually no market for an interest in the vehicle in the meantime.

A pooled vehicle gives you diversification only when it holds different assets. A fund holding 25 companies does, to a degree. A single-deal SPV never does, however it is structured. If you want a spread, you build it across several deals, sized so that losing any one of them is bearable.

New shares or existing shares

The vehicle looks the same from the outside whether it buys new shares or existing ones, but the deals differ in three practical ways.

New shares put the money in the company's bank account. Existing shares put it in the seller's pocket, and the company is no better funded afterwards.

The tax differs. Buying existing UK shares usually costs 0.5% in stamp duty or SDRT, so a £450,000 purchase on a stock transfer form would carry £2,250. And UK Enterprise Investment Scheme relief depends on an individual subscribing for shares issued to them, which a purchase from an existing holder is not.

The company may also have a say. Under the model articles for private companies, directors may refuse to register a share transfer, and many companies' own articles add further restrictions. Ask early whether the board has agreed, because a price agreed with a seller is worth little if the transfer never reaches the register.

Whether the form affects your tax relief

For UK investors hoping for EIS relief, the form of the vehicle can decide the question. The relief is written for individuals. A limited company that subscribes in its own name is the subscriber, so its shareholders cannot claim for those shares. The statute does look through a nominee to the individual behind it, and HMRC's manual says that the look-through does not reach investors in a partnership, because a partner owns a share of all the partnership's assets rather than particular shares.

Whether it counts as a fund

In the UK, calling something an SPV does not settle whether it is regulated as a fund. The test for a collective investment scheme asks whether participants share in the profits, lack day-to-day control of the property, and have their contributions pooled or the property managed as a whole by an operator. An exclusions order takes some arrangements out, including most bodies corporate, so a limited company is generally not a scheme, while a limited partnership, a limited liability partnership or a pooled nominee arrangement can be. Nothing in the test depends on how many assets there are, so holding one company is no answer by itself. Establishing or operating such a scheme is a specified activity, which generally needs FCA permission when done by way of business. Other countries draw these lines differently.

Four things to find on the first read

Before you sign, find the answers to these in the documents, not on the deal sheet:

  • What legal form is the vehicle, and what will you hold: shares, a beneficial interest in shares, or an interest in a partnership?
  • How much of your money reaches the company, and which costs come out later?
  • Who can agree to a sale, and what happens if investors disagree?
  • Are these new shares or existing ones, and if existing, has the company approved the transfer?
