How carry is worked out when an SPV exits

Carry is a percentage of profit, but the result depends on what counts as profit, what gets paid first, and which deals are counted together.

Updated 7 November 2026

A deal sheet usually gives carry a single line: "15% carry". That line settles the rate and leaves open almost everything that decides the amount. Three questions fill the gap. What has to come back to investors before there is any profit? Must investors earn a minimum return first? And do losses on other deals count? Running one exit through each question shows how far the answer can move.

The basic calculation

Bulma leads an SPV into Senzu Labs. Investors pay in £400,000, including £50,000 from Goku and £100,000 from Piccolo. Of that, £20,000 goes on setting up and running the vehicle, so £380,000 buys shares. Carry is 15%.

Five years later Senzu Labs is sold. The SPV receives £1,000,000 and pays £10,000 in exit costs, leaving £990,000 to distribute.

Step Amount
Available to distribute £990,000
Paid-in capital returned to investors £400,000
Profit £590,000
Carry to Bulma (15%) £88,500
Profit to investors (85%) £501,500
Total to investors £901,500

Goku holds one-eighth of the investor money and receives £112,687.50. Piccolo holds a quarter and receives £225,375.

If the shares had fetched only £350,000 after costs, there would be no profit and no carry. Goku would get £43,750 back on his £50,000.

What comes back first

The example returns all £400,000 of paid-in capital before counting profit. Some documents could instead define profit against the £380,000 that actually bought shares. Profit would then be £610,000 and carry £91,500. That £3,000 difference is 15% of the £20,000 in costs, and it falls on investors.

Neither version is built into the word "carry". Look for the definition, and check three related points: whether costs paid out of proceeds come off before the split, whether later top-ups for vehicle costs count as capital, and whether the lead's own money bears carry at all.

Hurdles and catch-up

A hurdle, or preferred return, means investors must reach a set return before the lead takes anything. Say Bulma's deal has an 8% hurdle, compounding annually for the five years. Investors must first receive £587,731: their £400,000 plus £187,731.

What happens next depends on the catch-up. With a full catch-up, every pound above the hurdle goes to the lead until she holds 15% of all profit paid so far. Here that takes £33,129. The remaining £369,140 is then split 85/15, giving her another £55,371. Her total is £88,500, exactly the figure with no hurdle at all.

So on a good exit, a full catch-up changes the order of payment and not the amount. Hurdles bite on modest exits, or where there is no catch-up and the lead takes 15% only of what exceeds the hurdle:

Available to distribute No hurdle 8% hurdle, full catch-up 8% hurdle, no catch-up
£450,000 £7,500 £0 £0
£600,000 £30,000 £12,269 £1,840
£990,000 £88,500 £88,500 £60,340

On the £450,000 line the deal made a profit, but not enough to clear the hurdle, so the lead receives nothing.

Practice varies by type of fund. In one study of 238 private equity funds raised between 1992 and 2006, 93.1% of buyout funds had a hurdle against 47.6% of venture funds, and 8% was the most common rate. Those are older institutional fund terms, and they say nothing about what a given SPV will offer.

Whether losses elsewhere count

An SPV stands alone, so a loss on another deal does not touch its carry. A fund can be different, because it can net all its investments together.

Suppose the same investors put £100,000 into each of three companies, with 15% carry.

Company Invested Returned Gain or loss Carry if each deal stands alone
First £100,000 £400,000 £300,000 £45,000
Second £100,000 £150,000 £50,000 £7,500
Third £100,000 £0 −£100,000 £0
Total £300,000 £550,000 £250,000 £52,500

Across three separate SPVs, the lead earns £52,500. In a single fund that nets everything, carry is 15% of £250,000, which is £37,500. The £15,000 gap is 15% of the £100,000 lost on the third company.

Funds also differ on timing. Under a whole-fund model, investors get back all contributions before carry is paid. Under a deal-by-deal model, a manager can be paid on an early winner before later losses are known, which is why such funds rely on a clawback: a promise to repay any excess at the end. A clawback is only as good as the manager's ability to pay it. ILPA, the institutional investors' association, recommends the whole-fund model as best practice. That is a recommendation and binds nobody until it is written into a fund's agreement.

What to pin down before closing

Whoever will run the numbers at exit needs each of these in writing before anyone invests: the rate; whether capital means paid-in or invested; how costs are treated; any hurdle rate, its compounding and start date; the catch-up percentage; and what happens to the lead's own stake. If the document is silent on one of them, the argument happens at exit, when real money depends on the answer.

Tax is a separate question from the calculation and depends on where the people involved are based. It can also turn on how long the investment was held.
