SPV costs: who pays for the vehicle, and how to show it

An SPV costs money to set up and keeps costing money every year until it is closed, so decide at the start who pays each cost and where the money for the later years will sit.

Updated 27 November 2026

Explore

Bulma is raising £400,000 for Senzu Labs through a new nominee company. Her first draft of the terms says "costs: 2%". That is a number, not an answer. It does not say what the 2% pays for, whether it comes on top of each investment or out of it, or what happens if the vehicle lasts longer than planned. An investor reading it in year eight will want all three.

Two kinds of cost

Some costs come once, at the start. Others recur for as long as the company exists.

Cost When Amount in this example
Legal documents for the SPV Once, at set-up £2,350
Incorporating the nominee company Once, at set-up £100
Confirmation statement Every year £50
Preparing and filing the accounts Every year £600
Closing the company at the end Once, at the end £1,000

The two Companies House figures are its published fees for filing online: £100 to incorporate and £50 for a confirmation statement, as listed in September 2026. A company has to deliver a confirmation statement for each review period of 12 months, and its directors must deliver accounts for each financial year, even if the company only holds one shareholding. The legal, accounts and closing figures are assumptions for the example. Get quotes, and ask an accountant whether VAT applies to any of them.

If Bulma expects the vehicle to last seven years, the expected total is £2,450 at the start, £650 × 7 = £4,550 over its life, and £1,000 at the end: £8,000, which is 2% of £400,000. So her 2% now has something behind it.

Four ways to pay

On top of each investment. Goku invests £100,000 and wires £102,000. His £100,000 buys shares and his £2,000 goes to costs. Every investor's investment is the round number they agreed.

Out of each investment. Goku wires £100,000, of which £98,000 buys shares and £2,000 goes to costs. Investors wire round numbers, but the amount invested is not.

Either way, where the round is under EIS, relief is given on amounts subscribed for shares, so the part of a payment that covers the vehicle's costs does not earn relief. An investor who wires £100,000 out of which £2,000 goes to costs should not expect relief on £100,000.

By the company. Senzu Labs agrees to pay the costs out of the money it raises. Investors' amounts do not change, and the company receives £392,000 instead of £400,000. That is the company's decision, and under EIS it is the riskiest route. A company that pays a cost the investors would otherwise owe, or gives them a benefit, gives them value, which can reduce or withdraw their relief. Where that is arranged before the shares are issued, even a small amount counts. The money raised must also be used for the company's qualifying business activity. Take tax advice before choosing this route for an EIS round.

By the lead. Bulma pays and keeps the deal attractive. Some leads take these costs back out of the exit proceeds before carry is worked out. If so, the documents should say so, because it changes what investors receive at exit.

Investors usually share costs in proportion to what they invest. A fixed amount per investor looks simpler but weighs very differently. At £1,000 each across eight investors, Goku's share is 1% of his £100,000 and Krillin's is 10% of his £10,000. Decide which basis is fair before the memo goes out, not after a small investor complains.

Keep costs apart from the lead's pay. A deal fee or carry rewards the lead; costs keep the vehicle running. Show them on separate lines, so nobody reads one as the other.

The money for later years

Under the "on top" route, Bulma collects £8,000 at closing. She spends £2,450 at once. The other £5,550 has to sit somewhere for seven years, and it should be visible.

Year 1 Amount
Reserve after set-up £5,550
Confirmation statement −£50
Accounts −£600
Reserve at year end £4,900

Three questions decide whether the reserve is enough and whose it is.

Who owns what is left. If the company is sold in year four, the annual costs for years five to seven, £650 × 3 = £1,950, are never needed. The £1,000 for closing is still spent, so £1,950 is left over. The documents should say whether it goes back to investors in proportion to what they paid in, and it should appear in the last distribution.

What happens if it runs out. If the vehicle lasts ten years instead of seven, there are three more years at £650: £1,950 that nobody collected. Fees can also rise. The usual answers are to take the shortfall from exit proceeds before anything is distributed, or for the lead to cover it. Asking eight investors to send small sums in year nine is the answer that does not work. Write down which one applies.

Where it is held. If the documents treat the reserve as investors' money held for a purpose, keep it traceable to the bank statement, and report it once a year with the opening figure, what was spent and the closing figure.

How to show it to investors

Each investor's closing statement should let them rebuild their own number: what they wired, what was invested, what went to costs, and how their share of costs splits between set-up and the reserve. For Goku that is £102,000 wired, £100,000 invested, £2,000 to costs, of which £612.50 paid for set-up and £1,387.50 is his share of the reserve.

Then repeat the reserve table once a year, in the same layout. If nothing was spent beyond the plan, say so in one line. If a cost changed, say which, by how much, and what that does to the years remaining.
