Committing to a venture fund: the promise behind the first payment

A fund commitment is a promise to hand over money on demand for years, so plan for the whole figure rather than the first call.

Updated 7 November 2026

Buying a listed share is finished in a moment: you pay, you own it. A venture fund works the other way round. On the day you sign you own almost nothing and have paid little or nothing, but you have agreed to send money whenever the manager asks, up to a stated limit, into companies nobody can yet name.

What you have promised

The limit is your commitment. The manager draws it in instalments, called capital calls, as investments and costs come up. What you have not yet paid is your undrawn commitment, and it stays a live obligation until the fund's documents say otherwise.

The law can be blunt about this. Under Delaware's limited partnership statute, a partner must perform a promise to contribute even if unable to because of death or disability, unless the partnership agreement says otherwise. The same section lets the agreement set consequences for missing a payment: a reduced or eliminated interest, a forced sale, or outright forfeiture. Funds formed elsewhere sit under different statutes, so the default clause in your own agreement is the thing to read, and to read before you sign.

How long you are in

In a study of 238 private equity and venture funds, the typical fund ran for ten years. New companies could be added in roughly the first five; the rest was for follow-on rounds and selling. Your agreement states its own term and any extensions, and extensions matter because a fund cannot always sell its last holdings on schedule.

The SEC's guide to private equity funds says it plainly: investors may wait several years before any return, and withdrawal rights are typically limited. Treat the money as gone for a decade, with the outflows arriving unevenly.

A commitment on a timeline

Suppose Piccolo commits £250,000.

Year Event Paid or received Undrawn afterwards
1 Call Pays £50,000 £200,000
2 Call Pays £62,500 £137,500
3 Call Pays £37,500 £100,000
4 Early sale, distribution Receives £20,000 £100,000, or £120,000 if recallable
5 Call Pays £60,000 £40,000, or £60,000 if recallable

Two things stand out. In year 3 Piccolo has paid £150,000 and has seen nothing back, and the fund still has a claim on another £100,000. And year 4 shows why the word "recallable" deserves attention. Many agreements allow recycling, where proceeds are added back to the undrawn commitment and can be called again. That £20,000 looked like a return, but the fund may be able to ask for it again. Do not plan on spending a distribution until you know which kind it is.

The practical test is simple. Could you fund the whole undrawn figure from cash or assets you could sell in a bad year, when the listed market is down and your other income is under pressure? If the honest answer depends on the fund paying you first, the commitment is too large.

What reaches companies

Not all of the £250,000 buys shares. Take a fee of 2% a year on commitments, held flat for ten years. That is £5,000 a year and £50,000 over the life of the fund, leaving £200,000 to invest. The study above uses this same pattern to show a fee taking 20% of committed capital, though many funds step the fee down after the investment period. Add fund expenses, then carry on the profit at the end.

So ask to see what you get back under a few outcomes, written in cash. If the portfolio returns 2.5 times the £200,000 invested, that is £500,000 before carry; at 20% carry on the £250,000 profit, £50,000 goes to the manager and £450,000 to you. That is 1.8 times your money, against a headline 2.5. The exact mechanics, including any preferred return and the order of payments, come from the agreement.

Who actually holds the interest

You might not be the limited partner at all. A feeder vehicle or a fund of funds can sit between you and the fund, and then its name is on the register, its terms stack on top of the fund's, and its calls are the ones you answer. Before subscribing, draw the chain from your bank account to the portfolio company and write the fee at each step.

Access also varies by country. In the US, private equity funds are typically open only to accredited investors and qualified clients; the accredited investor tests for individuals include net worth over $1 million excluding your main home, or income over $200,000 ($300,000 jointly) in each of the last two years, with the same expected this year. Qualified client status is a separate, higher test. In the UK, section 238 of the Financial Services and Markets Act 2000 restricts promotion of unregulated collective investment schemes, so the manager's documents should tell you which exemption brings you within reach.

Questions worth asking before you sign

Most of what matters can be found by putting a few specific requests to the manager:

  • What is the track record, in cash returned as well as in valuations, and how much of it rests on one or two holdings?
  • Who made those past decisions, and do they still work here?
  • What happens to the fund if a named partner leaves?
  • What is the notice period for a call, and what is the cure period if a payment is late?
  • Can I transfer my interest, whose consent do I need, and does my buyer take on my undrawn commitment?

ILPA's due diligence questionnaire is a longer list to borrow from, although its user guide says it was not designed with venture capital in mind, so expect some questions not to fit.

After you are in

Keep a running sheet: commitment, paid in, received, recallable, undrawn. Reconcile it against every notice and report. When a call arrives, confirm the bank details through a contact you already hold, not through the email carrying the instruction; a request for a large transfer on a deadline is exactly what a fraudster would imitate.

Then name a person who can act when you cannot. Calls do not wait for holidays, and a ten-year promise will outlast at least one change in your circumstances.
