Late-stage deals: from headline valuation to the cash you receive

A big valuation describes one class of share at one date, so work out what your own shares would receive at several sale prices before calling a price cheap.

Updated 7 November 2026

A company at this stage has real revenue and a name people recognise, which removes some risks. What it does not remove is the risk of overpaying for the particular share you are buying.

A worked discount

Senzu Labs has grown up. Its last round sold preferred shares at £25, giving a headline valuation of £500 million on 20 million shares. A holder offers Bulma's SPV ordinary shares at £17.50, advertised as "30% off the last round".

The arithmetic is right. The comparison may not be. Assume all 6 million preferred shares (30%) have identical rights: a non-participating preference of £20 a share, £120 million in total, or one-for-one conversion into ordinary shares, whichever pays more at a sale. The other 14 million shares are ordinary. Now look at what ordinary shares receive:

Sale price Preferred takes Left for ordinary Per ordinary share Result at £17.50
£100m £100m £0 £0 Total loss
£300m £120m £180m £12.86 Loss
£400m £120m (converting is worth the same) £280m £20.00 1.14x
£500m £150m (converts) £350m £25.00 1.43x
£800m £240m (converts) £560m £40.00 2.29x

Below £400 million, the preference gives the preferred holders more than their 30% share of the sale, and the extra comes out of the ordinary shares. The buyer breaks even when ordinary shares get £17.50 each: £245 million for ordinary plus the £120 million preference, a £365 million sale. So the discount holds up against a 27% fall from the headline, but not against a sharper one.

Real companies are messier. Several series can have different preferences, some senior to others, and each new round can add to the stack. Debt ranks ahead of all of it, and options change the share count. So ask for the company's actual charter terms and capitalisation, and redo this table. The NVCA's model financing documents, a common US starting point, show how many separate agreements these rights can be spread across.

Time changes the answer

A late-stage holding may be priced for a listing "next year" that keeps moving. Compare outcomes in annual rates as well as multiples:

Outcome Multiple Years Annual return
Sold soon 2x 3 about 26%
Sold later for more 3x 8 about 14.7%

The bigger multiple is the worse rate. For an SPV with a fixed life and investors expecting their money back, the slower route also adds years of running costs.

Is the reference price any good?

Even a correct comparison needs a fair reference. How old is that £25? A round priced two years ago tells you little about what the same shares are worth now, and a private company's last price can stand for a long time simply because no new round has reset it.

Consider who is on the other side as well. Someone close to the company has chosen to sell. There may be a sound reason, such as paying a tax bill or buying a house, but they know more than you. Ask what information you will get before the purchase and after it. If the company gives secondary buyers no financial reporting, you will be valuing your holding blind.

Where the money goes, and what the business needs

In a primary round the company issues new shares and keeps the money. In a secondary, an existing holder sells and keeps the money, and the company gets nothing. A deal can be a mix, so ask which yours is. Money paid to a departing employee funds no growth, and you should not value it as if it did.

The sale prices in the table are guesses, so test them against how the business makes money. Split revenue growth by source: new customers, more from existing customers, price rises and acquisitions. Check how much comes from the top few customers. Follow "adjusted" profit back to the cash that actually moved. Then ask how long the current cash lasts and how much more funding it will take to reach a sale or a listing, because new preferred money may well rank ahead of you.

How the stake is held

If an SPV holds the shares, find out who is on the register. Ask who can accept a tender offer, who decides when to sell after a listing, and what fees and carry apply. UK investors should also note that EIS and SEIS relief needs newly issued shares paid for in cash, so buying existing shares from a holder does not meet the condition, whatever the company's status.

A listing does not settle it

An IPO can remove the preference issue. Preferred shares usually convert into ordinary shares, and the NVCA model charter ties mandatory conversion to a defined "qualified IPO". Check the threshold in your company's actual charter, because a listing that falls short of it may need a holder vote to convert.

Converting does not produce cash either. Holders usually agree to a lock-up, typically 180 days in the US, during which the shares cannot be sold, and prices can fall when many shares become sellable at once. The valuation at listing and the money you finally receive are different figures, and only the second one belongs in your table.
