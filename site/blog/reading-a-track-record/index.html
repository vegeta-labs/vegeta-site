What a fund's track record can and cannot tell you

Past returns in venture carry real information, but only if you look at what was known when the decision was made.

Updated 7 November 2026

Bulma is raising her second fund. Her first is three years old, and most of it is unsold. Investors deciding now can see a TVPI of 1.3x and little else. Seven years later that first fund finishes at 3.5x, thanks to one late exit. Anyone looking back will file Fund II as the successor to a top performer. Nobody could have known that when Fund II was raising.

This gap is the main problem with reading track records. A final ranking tells you how a fund ended. An investor has to decide on the figures that exist on the day.

What the research found

The clearest test is a 2020 paper by Harris, Jenkinson, Kaplan and Stucke, which uses institutional investors' cash flows from Burgiss on US buyout and venture funds. They measured a manager's previous fund in two ways: on its final performance and on what was visible at the time of fundraising.

On final performance, returns persist. Venture funds whose previous fund ended in the top quartile landed in the top quartile again 45% of the time, against the 25% that chance would suggest.

Using only what was known at fundraising, the picture weakens. For buyouts, persistence largely disappears after 2000. For venture it survives, but it is smaller.

US venture funds, sorted by previous fund's PME at fundraising Average PME, all funds Average PME, post-2000 funds
Previous fund in top quartile 1.70 1.20
Previous fund in second quartile 1.33 1.15
Previous fund in bottom quartile 0.91 0.91
First-time fund 1.24 1.23

PME here is a public market equivalent against the S&P 500, where anything above 1 beat the index. Three points stand out.

The advantage after 2000 is much thinner. Across all four quartiles the post-2000 difference is not statistically significant, and the gap between top and bottom reaches significance only at the 10% level.

First-time funds did not lag. After 2000 their average was slightly above, though not significantly above, that of funds following a top-quartile predecessor. Excluding new managers on principle finds little support here.

Second-quartile predecessors still beat the index on average, which sits awkwardly with the idea that only top-quartile funds are worth backing.

Why the timing changes the picture

Interim marks are only moderately linked to final results, and managers choose when to raise. In the same paper's venture sample, 77 funds were raised while the previous fund sat in the bottom quartile, yet 118 ended up following a fund that finished there. Weak performers tend to delay raising or never raise again, and managers tend to raise when marks look good.

So an investor should expect a pitch to arrive at a flattering moment. The useful request is for dated reports: what the previous fund showed at each quarter-end, and how those marks later turned out.

What might be producing the repeat

Persistence does not by itself show that a manager is good at picking companies. Nanda, Samila and Sorenson studied individual investments and found each additional IPO among a firm's first ten investments predicted as much as an 8% higher IPO rate on its later ones, an effect that fades with time. Early success, they found, stemmed in large part from being in the right places at the right times, and firms did not persist in that. What did change was that successful firms went on to invest in later rounds and larger syndicates. The authors read this as better access to deals.

If that explanation is right, the question moves from "was this team skilled?" to "does this team still have what got it into those deals?" An IPO count also differs from an investor's net return, which depends on price, stake size, fees and timing.

Questions for any record

Who did the work? A partner who led the winning deal may have left. A lead's list of past investments may include deals where they were a passenger. Ask who sourced each deal and who made the decision, and whether those people are in the room.

Is the list complete? A track record built from a selected set of deals proves little. Ask for every investment made under the strategy, including write-offs, each with dates and amounts.

Is the record realised? A 3x made up of cash is different from a 3x made up of a single unsold company marked at its last round. Ask for DPI alongside TVPI, and for the largest holding as a share of remaining value.

Is it net? A deal multiple and an investor's net return can be far apart once fees, costs and carry are taken out.

Is the next vehicle comparable? A manager who made early-stage returns from a small fund may not repeat them with a fund five times larger, where the same ownership costs more and each exit has to be bigger to matter. A move into a new stage or sector has the same problem.

Applying this to a syndicate lead

An SPV investor usually sees a list of past deals and not a fund report, so the same questions apply with fewer safeguards. Often no auditor has checked that list, and a recent up-round can make a young portfolio look proven. Ask how many of the deals have returned money, and what investors actually received after carry and costs.

Treat the record as a set of questions about how a result was produced and whether the conditions still hold. The research supports taking it seriously. It does not support reading it as a forecast.
