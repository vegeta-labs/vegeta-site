The closing book: what to tell a founder when they ask how much you have

A single total hides the only thing a founder needs to know, which is how much of the money will actually arrive and when.

Updated 7 November 2026

Six days before closing, the founder of Senzu Labs asks Bulma how the round is going. Bulma's allocation is £500,000. Her spreadsheet says £465,000. Both numbers are true, and neither is an answer, because neither says how much money can reach the company by Friday.

The fix is a closing book: every investor in exactly one stage, moving forward only when something has really happened.

Sort the money by what has happened

Stage Amount What it takes to move on
Funded: documents complete, checks done, cash matched to the bank statement £310,000 Closing conditions
Signed: documents complete, cash not yet in £95,000 Payment, then matching
Promised: said yes, nothing signed £60,000 Checks, documents, payment
Total £465,000

Each row also needs a name, a date and the one thing outstanding. Two rules keep it honest. An investor is in one row only, so the same person never gets counted twice. And money counts as funded only once it has been matched to a specific investor on a bank statement, because "I sent it yesterday" belongs in the second row.

What the founder gets is three short lines: "£310,000 is in and clean. Another £95,000 is signed and due by Wednesday. I have £60,000 of verbal interest I am not counting yet." They can plan around that.

The target is bigger than the allocation

The £500,000 is what the company should receive. Investors have to pay in that plus the cost of running the vehicle, so the target needs working out before anyone quotes it.

If costs are a fixed £12,500, the target is simple: £512,500.

If costs are a percentage of the money raised, simple addition is wrong. At 2.5% of subscriptions, adding 2.5% of £500,000 gives £512,500, but 2.5% of that is £12,812.50, leaving the company £312.50 short. The right sum divides instead:

£500,000 ÷ 0.975 = £512,820.51

Costs at 2.5% of that are £12,820.51, leaving exactly £500,000. On a bigger raise or with a higher percentage the gap grows, and it tends to surface on the last day. Many fee schedules also have a minimum or a cap, so run the sum on the actual schedule at the actual raise size.

Bulma's fee is fixed, so her target is £512,500. Against that, her book looks different. If everything signed arrives she has £405,000, which is £107,500 short. Even if every verbal promise comes through she has £465,000, which is £47,500 short. The total on her spreadsheet had been hiding the fact that she has a problem, and six days is still enough time to act on it.

When there is too little

Settle the options while there is time to use them, and make sure the founder hears about them from you. Usually there are four:

  • Close at a smaller number, if the company agrees and a minimum in the documents allows it.
  • Extend the date, if the company and the documents allow it.
  • Bring in another eligible investor, who needs checks and documents like anyone else.
  • Cancel under the terms and return the money.

Fixed costs matter here. £12,500 is 2.5% of a £500,000 raise but 5% of a £250,000 one, so there is a point below which the deal stops making sense for investors. Work out that minimum before the raise opens. A lead should also be careful not to imply they will personally fill any gap, unless they have actually agreed to.

When there is too much

Now suppose Senzu Labs is popular and Bulma has £640,625 of eligible demand against a £512,500 cap. The fair-looking answer is to scale everyone by the same ratio:

£512,500 ÷ £640,625 = 0.8

Goku's £50,000 becomes £40,000 and Piccolo's £25,000 becomes £20,000.

That is one policy among several. Others include honouring earlier written promises in full, protecting a minimum ticket, or favouring investors who funded first. Any of these can be reasonable. What causes trouble is choosing after you have seen who is affected, so write the rule down early and tell investors what it is before they commit.

Scaling then creates three chores that are easy to forget.

Rounding. Companies issue whole shares. At £3.20 a share, with £500,000 of every £512,500 reaching the company, Goku's £40,000 buys £39,024.39 of shares, or 12,195.12 of them. He gets 12,195, costing £39,024.00, and £0.39 is left over. Across thirty investors, the leftovers need a written rule too.

Refunds. If Goku paid his full £50,000 up front, £10,000 goes back to him, to the account it came from. Track it separately from capital, as money owed to him and not part of the raise. If he had only committed and not yet paid, his call simply becomes £40,000.

Records. The signed documents, the register and the accounts should all show £40,000. The number he first asked for belongs in the history and nowhere else.

Set your dates backwards

Start from the company's completion date and count back. Payment release needs final reconciliation before it. Reconciliation needs money in. Money needs time to clear, particularly from abroad. Investors need checks and signed documents before they send it, and a new investor may take days to clear them, especially one investing through a company or trust.

Set a last date for new investors well ahead of the last date for funds, and leave the final days for the short list of named exceptions. Nobody should be starting onboarding for your largest ticket on the last afternoon.

Before releasing anything, the same four numbers must agree: accepted subscriptions, cash matched on the bank statement, costs, and the amount going to the company. Someone other than the preparer should check them, and record that they did.

After closing, compare the book at each date with what eventually arrived. Over a few deals you will learn how much of "promised" tends to turn into "funded" among your own investors, which makes the next conversation with a founder more honest.
