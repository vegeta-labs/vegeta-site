Companies House identity checks and the syndicate SPV

Who has to verify their identity when a syndicate's SPV or nominee is a UK company, what applies now that the transition year has ended, and what the check leaves untouched.

Updated 27 November 2026

Explore

Since 18 November 2025, directors and people with significant control of UK companies have had to prove to Companies House that they are who they say they are. The duty comes from the Economic Crime and Corporate Transparency Act 2023. A syndicate lead who sets up a company for each deal meets this rule every time.

Who has to verify

The current list covers directors and their equivalents in other entity types, people with significant control (PSCs), and the agents authorised to carry out checks for others. For a company, a PSC is broadly someone who holds more than 25% of the shares or voting rights, can appoint or remove a majority of the directors, or otherwise has significant influence or control.

Shareholders as such are not on the list. Nor, yet, are several groups that Companies House says will follow later: people who file documents, limited partnerships, corporate directors and the officers of a company that is itself a PSC.

Where the dates stand in late November 2026

The rule started with a 12-month transition year for people already on the register, which ended on 17 November 2026. Existing directors were due with the confirmation statement falling between 18 November 2025 and 17 November 2026. Existing PSCs who were also directors had 14 days tied to that statement, and other existing PSCs had the first 14 days of their birth month. Companies House now says that transition period has passed. Individual deadlines can still fall after the year ended, because the transitional rules count from each company's own dates. A company whose confirmation statement review period ended on 17 November 2026 ordinarily has until 1 December to deliver the statement, and the 14 days for a director who is also its PSC run from 18 November to 1 December. An older SPV whose director has still not verified is overdue or about to be: take the date from the service.

For anything new, three rules have applied since the first day:

  • a new company cannot be incorporated until its directors have verified;
  • a new director cannot be appointed to an existing company without verifying;
  • a new PSC must verify within 14 days.

Two things are still to come. Companies House says it will place notes on the public register where verification was missed. And the transition plan puts identity checks on everyone who files a document, and a requirement that third-party filing agents be authorised, at no earlier than November 2027, with at least six months' notice.

Two routes, one personal code

A person can verify directly through GOV.UK One Login, which Companies House describes as free, with a Post Office option for those who need it. Or they can go through an Authorised Corporate Service Provider: an accountant, solicitor or similar agent registered with Companies House and supervised for anti-money laundering. An agent may charge.

Either way the result is a personal code of 11 characters. It belongs to the individual, not to any company, and one code serves every appointment that person holds. A director gives it at incorporation or with the appointment filing. A PSC gives it when first added to the register or within 14 days, through a separate PSC service that shows the exact dates and allows a 14-day extension if it is requested before the deadline.

Most people verify once and should not repeat it unless Companies House asks. The code is needed for each role a person holds, so someone who is both director and PSC of one company supplies it twice.

A worked example

Bulma's syndicate raises £400,000 for a 20% stake in Senzu Labs: Goku £160,000, Piccolo £120,000, Krillin £80,000 and Tien £40,000.

Suppose the SPV is a new company with Bulma as sole director and ordinary shares issued in proportion to the money. Goku holds 40% and Piccolo 30%, so on the share test both are PSCs of the SPV. Krillin at 20% and Tien at 10% are not. Three people need a personal code: Bulma before the company can be formed, Goku and Piccolo within their 14 days. Collecting all three codes before incorporation avoids finding out afterwards that one of them is missing.

Now suppose a nominee company owned and run by Bulma holds the 20% for the four investors. Bulma is director and PSC of the nominee, and gives her code in both roles. A company that acts as nominee shareholder by way of business is within the Money Laundering Regulations' definition of a trust or company service provider, which is one more reason to take advice before running your own nominee. For the PSC test at Senzu Labs, the Companies Act treats a share held by a nominee as held by the person behind it. Goku's share is 40% of 20%, which is 8% of Senzu Labs; Piccolo's is 6%, Krillin's 4% and Tien's 2%. On these facts no investor meets the share test, so none has to verify with Companies House. Voting arrangements or other rights can change that answer, which is a question for the lawyer who drafts the documents.

If someone does not verify

An individual must not act as a director unless verified, and the company must make sure nobody does. Breach is an offence for the individual, and for the company and every officer in default, though the section leaves the director's acts valid. Companies House's stated approach includes financial penalties, prosecution and referral to the Insolvency Service, and it says PSCs who do not verify may also commit an offence.

In September 2026 the Insolvency Service secured its first convictions for these offences. Three directors were fined, one of them a verified director who had not taken reasonable steps to stop an unverified colleague acting.

What the check does not do

It confirms that a named person exists and is that person. That is all.

It is not due diligence on investors. Firms covered by the Money Laundering Regulations must, among other things, identify beneficial owners, understand the purpose of the relationship and keep monitoring it. A personal code answers none of those questions. Whether those regulations apply to a lead, or to the provider acting for the syndicate, is a matter for professional advice, like the separate question of FCA permission.

Nor does it reach every investor behind a nominee. The PSC rules do look through nominee holdings, but only a person who meets a PSC condition has to verify, and in the example nobody does.

What to record for each deal

Keep one table per company, with a row for each role:

  1. the company and the role (director, PSC, or both as two rows);
  2. the person;
  3. the route used, and the agent's name if there was one;
  4. the date verified;
  5. the date the code was given to Companies House, and the due date shown in the service;
  6. any extension requested and its new date.

Add a row for whoever files, ready for the rule on filers. Review the table at each confirmation statement and whenever the share split changes, because an investor who crosses 25% in a follow-on round becomes a PSC with a 14-day clock.
