Buying a venture stake second-hand: the price is the easy part

A secondary hands you someone else's position, so budget for what comes with it, and look past the discount to what you are really taking on.

Updated 7 November 2026

A secondary is a sale between holders. The company or fund receives nothing, and the buyer steps into the seller's place, including the awkward parts. A headline of "90% of NAV" tells you very little about any of that, so start with what the cheque really comes to.

The sum most buyers underestimate

Fund interests are usually priced as a percentage of net asset value on a reference date, while the deal closes months later. In between, the fund keeps moving, so the contract has to say who bears what. A common approach gives the buyer the economics from the reference date, which means the price moves with cash flows after it.

Say Bulma's SPV agrees to pay 90% of a fund interest with a £400,000 reference value, and £80,000 is undrawn.

Item Amount
Base price: 90% of £400,000 £360,000
Add: call paid by seller after the reference date +£30,000
Less: distribution received by seller after the reference date −£12,000
Paid at closing £378,000
Undrawn commitment now: £80,000 − £30,000 £50,000
Total the buyer must be able to fund £428,000

The budget is £428,000, not £360,000. Adjusting the reference value in the same way gives £418,000 (£400,000 + £30,000 − £12,000), and £378,000 is about 90.4% of it, so the percentage moved slightly too. Agreements differ on exactly which items adjust the price, so go through them line by line and get evidence for each cash flow from the manager or administrator.

What a discount does not tell you

A price of 90% of NAV compares the deal with one estimate on one date. It says nothing about what you will receive. So ask how old the valuations are, and which holdings make up most of the number. If a single company is half the value, you are making a concentrated bet on that company, and you should look at it directly.

Then allow for what comes out first: remaining fees, the manager's carry, and any preferences in the portfolio companies. Time counts as well. Late in a fund's life there may be extensions ahead, and each year costs something.

Share deals call for the same care. Ordinary shares offered against the price of a recent preferred round may be worth quite a bit less, for reasons the discount does not capture.

Also ask what you will be told after closing. Without regular information, you will struggle to value the stake or decide what to do with it.

Two different purchases

When you buy shares, the core risk is title and rights. Does the seller own these shares, in this class, free of any pledge? Does the company's register agree? Do the shares carry the rights you think you are paying for?

When you buy a limited partner's interest in a fund, you are taking over a contract. You get a share of the remaining holdings along with fees still to come and possibly a duty to fund future calls. Under Delaware's partnership statute, a buyer who becomes a limited partner generally takes on the seller's contribution obligations, subject to the agreement and except for those unknown to the buyer that could not be found from the agreement, while the seller is not released from its own liability. So the transfer documents should state who stands behind what. Other jurisdictions have their own rules, and the fund's partnership agreement applies either way.

The same section says a buyer becomes a limited partner only as the agreement provides or, failing that, with the consent of all partners. Paying for an interest does not by itself make you a partner.

If a vehicle holds the asset, go one layer deeper. Buying from an SPV member means dealing with the SPV's rules, its register and its lead.

Who has to say yes

Sales rarely complete on the buyer's and seller's word alone. For shares, look for transfer limits in the articles and shareholder agreements. The US NVCA model suite, for example, has a separate right of first refusal and co-sale agreement. Under terms like these the company or other holders may be able to take the deal at the agreed price, or sell alongside the seller, so a buyer can spend weeks on diligence and still lose the shares.

For fund interests, the general partner's consent is the usual gate, and it may come with investor checks and legal costs. Ask whether the seller's side letter passes to you, because reduced fees or extra reporting may stop at the transfer.

Finish with evidence, not just a signed contract: approvals received, the register or partnership records updated, the price reconciled, notices redirected to you, and a schedule of what you still owe. Until the records show your name, the fund's next notice is going to the seller.

Tax at closing

Tax follows the asset and the people. In the UK, stamp duty is usually 0.5% on shares transferred by stock transfer form when the price is over £1,000. For US partnership interests, a buyer may have to withhold 10% of the amount realised when a foreign seller's gain would be effectively connected with a US business, and the partnership may have to withhold from later distributions if the buyer fails to. The amount realised can include liabilities, so settle this before signing.

When the manager is on both sides

In a continuation deal, a general partner moves assets from an old fund to a new vehicle it also runs, and existing investors choose to sell or roll. The manager is on both sides of the price, and your role may be seller, roller or new buyer.

ILPA's October 2026 guidance is a recommendation rather than a rule, and it sets a useful bar. It asks for a competitive process or other independent price validation, supported by an independent fairness opinion or similar. It asks for an election period of generally at least 20 clear business days from receiving the final package, and no overall increase in management fee liability for investors who roll.

Measure any offer against those points. If you have ten days and no data room, that tells you something.
