Where your deals really come from: a sourcing log for syndicate leads

A short log tells a syndicate lead which relationships produce investable deals, and where the rest fall away.

Updated 7 November 2026

Ask a lead where their best deals came from and you will get a story. Ask for the numbers and there usually aren't any. The log that produces them takes minutes per company, and after a year it starts to change how you spend your week.

The base rate is sobering

One large survey asked almost 900 institutional venture capitalists at 681 firms how they work. In the working-paper version, firms considered roughly 100 opportunities for each deal closed. Over 30% of closed deals came through professional networks, 20% from other investors, 8% from portfolio companies and almost 30% were generated by the firm itself. Only about 10% arrived inbound from company management.

The same paper found little difference in sources between firms with higher and lower IPO rates, which is a useful warning against assuming one channel is the secret. These are institutional funds, not syndicates. Treat the figures as context and collect your own.

What to record

Make the company the record, with one row each, however many people mention it. Add every introduction to that row with its date, because two different people can matter: whoever first told you about the company and whoever later got you allocation. Counting each introduction as a new opportunity inflates the channel that gossips most.

Then give each stage a definition you could explain to someone else. Syndicate leads need two stages that funds usually don't.

  • Seen: you know the company exists and have the basic facts.
  • In scope: it passes your stage, sector, geography and cheque rules.
  • First call: you spoke to a founder.
  • Deep look: you have a list of questions and have started answering them.
  • Allocation offered: the company or round lead has told you a figure.
  • Filled: enough investors committed to your SPV by the deadline.
  • Closed: the money reached the company.

A fund with committed capital can write the cheque once it decides to. A lead can win allocation and still fail to fill it, and that failure has a different cure from a sourcing problem. It belongs in the log.

A year of it

Suppose Bulma's log for one year reads like this, with every company first seen in that year and all decisions now made:

Stage Founders she has backed Other leads Inbound
Seen 30 45 60
In scope 12 15 5
First call 8 9 3
Deep look 5 3 1
Allocation offered 3 2 0
Filled and closed 2 1 0
Hours spent 90 70 40

Founder introductions closed 2 of 30, or 6.7%. Other leads closed 1 of 45, or 2.2%. Inbound closed none of 60. Per closed deal, she spent 45 hours on founder introductions and 70 on other leads.

Resist the verdict that founder introductions are three times better. There are three closed deals in the whole table. One more close from other leads would take that channel to 4.4%. A year of a syndicate's numbers can't rank channels by outcome, let alone by return, since none of these companies has exited.

The earlier rows have more data and are more useful. 40% of founder introductions were in scope and 33% of other leads' deals, against about 8% of inbound. So inbound's problem is targeting: 55 of 60 companies should never have written to her. That can be fixed cheaply with a clearer public statement of what she looks at, or a form that asks for stage and sector first.

Other leads produced nine first calls but only three deep looks. Either those deals are weak or she is taking calls out of politeness. The answer changes how she handles the next introduction.

And one of the two allocations from other leads, like one of the three from founders, never completed. That points away from sourcing altogether, towards how quickly she can reach her investors, or to whether that kind of deal suits them.

Why deals stop

Add one field saying why each company left the log, from a short fixed list:

  • Outside the rules
  • Didn't believe the case
  • Price
  • Terms of the instrument
  • No allocation available
  • Founder chose other investors
  • Couldn't fill the SPV
  • Ran out of time

Allow a line of free text as well, since "price" can hide very different stories, from a valuation that broke the return maths to a late change in the round.

Keep "no" separate from "not good". A strong team outside your rules is not a bad company, and a founder you admire who wants a different investor is not a judgement on either of you. A log that treats every exit as a negative mark will mislead you when that founder comes back two years later. "Ran out of time" deserves particular attention. If it appears often, the bottleneck is you.

Looking back without flattering yourself

Twice a year, pull a handful of companies you passed on. A later funding announcement proves very little. The new investors paid a different price for a different security on better evidence, and nobody knows yet whether they will make money. The useful question is what you could have known on the day.

The patterns are worth more than any single miss: promising companies screened out by a rule that turned out to be irrelevant, a good source who stopped sending deals because they never heard back, or a deep look so slow that the round had closed by the time it ended.

What to do with it

At each review, make three decisions in writing: which source gets more of your time, which one needs changing and which you will drop. Keep a small allowance for trying something new, with a date on which you will judge it.

Conversion is only one reading. A channel can close a high share of its deals because it sends you obvious companies at full prices. A very high close rate may also mean you have stopped saying no. Sourcing is working when suitable companies, at prices you can defend and with allocation you can get, keep arriving in numbers that let you turn most of them down.
