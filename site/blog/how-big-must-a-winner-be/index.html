How big must a winner be? Sizing a venture portfolio

A few companies produce most venture returns, so a portfolio has to be built to catch one and to own enough of it to matter.

Updated 7 November 2026

The number of companies in a portfolio is an output of the budget and not a starting preference. It has to reconcile how skewed outcomes are, how much money is actually available, and how much of a company each cheque buys.

How skewed outcomes are

Kerr, Nanda and Rhodes-Kropf looked at US start-ups that received a first early-stage venture round between 1985 and 2009. About 55% were terminated at a loss. Only about 6% returned more than five times the money invested, yet that 6% accounted for about half of all gross returns. Those figures are for companies across the market, before fund fees, and rely on estimates for exits with unreported values.

Investors call this the power law. Strictly, a power law is a particular mathematical distribution, and not every skewed set of returns follows one. The practical point holds either way: an average outcome tells you little, because a handful of results carry the total.

Start with the money that can buy shares

Take a £12m fund. Suppose lifetime fees and costs come to £2m, leaving £10m to invest. Holding back £3m for follow-ons leaves £7m for first cheques.

At that point the fund can write 20 cheques of £350,000 each or 40 cheques of £175,000. It is not free to do both, and any promise about ownership has to fit into the same budget.

Two questions, in opposite directions

Breadth raises the chance of holding a big winner. Using the 6% figure as a rough input, and pretending each company's fate is independent, the chance of holding at least one such company is 1 minus 0.94 raised to the number of companies.

Size decides whether the winner matters. A fivefold return on one cheque adds much less to a fund whose cheques are small.

20 companies 40 companies
Cheque £350,000 £175,000
Chance of at least one 5x-plus company 71% 92%
Proceeds from one 5x £1.75m £875,000
That 5x as a share of the £12m fund 14.6% 7.3%

Doubling the portfolio lifts the chance of holding such a company from 71% to 92%, while cutting what each one is worth to the fund in half. A single 5x does not make a fund either way. To reach even the fund's own size, the portfolio needs either several winners or something much larger than 5x.

Work back from ownership

Next, ask what one company would have to be worth to return the entire fund. Assume every cheque goes in at a £7m post-money valuation, and later rounds dilute the stake by 30% in total. Ignore preference shares and fees for the moment.

20 companies 40 companies
Stake bought 5% 2.5%
Stake at exit 3.5% 1.75%
Exit value to return £12m about £343m about £686m

This is the number that tests the plan. Does the fund's sector plausibly produce companies of that size, at those entry prices? The market for the product is not enough to answer that. What matters is the stake the fund will still hold at exit.

Entry price works the same way. If valuations move from £7m to £10m post-money, a £350,000 cheque buys 3.5% and not 5%. To keep 5%, the cheque rises to £500,000, and the same £7m now buys 14 companies. So a manager cannot choose these freely: at a given entry price, the target stake sets the cheque, and the budget then sets the number of companies.

Independence is the weak assumption

That probability table treats every company as a separate coin toss. Real portfolios are not like that. Companies selling to the same buyers, relying on the same platform or needing the same kind of follow-on capital can fail together, and a funding drought hits them all at once.

So 40 companies from one narrow sector, one network and one year may not offer much more real diversification than 20. A better count is the number of different things that would have to go wrong. List the shared dependencies, such as buyer budgets, a distribution channel, a regulator or next-round financing, and see how many holdings each one touches.

Specialists accept some of this deliberately, because knowledge of a market can improve selection. That trade is reasonable if it is explicit. Time diversification counts too: a fund investing over several years spreads its entry prices, while a fund that deploys in one hot year does not.

People are part of the budget

Each company adds work that continues long after the cheque. There are decisions to make on follow-ons, board or investor updates to read, consents to sign and capital table changes to record, for as long as the holding lasts. If 40 positions leave no time for proper selection, the odds in that table no longer apply, because they assumed the extra companies were as good as the first ones.

The same applies across a set of SPVs. A lead who raises deal by deal has no committed pool to budget from, but a rising count of live vehicles still means more statements, more investor questions and more corporate actions to handle every year.

A test for any plan

Write down the fund size, costs, follow-on allocation, cheque, number of companies, entry valuation, expected dilution and the exit needed to return the fund. Then run it again with a smaller close and a higher entry price. A plan that survives both, and still leaves time to do the work, is one the manager can actually keep to. Skew in venture returns argues for taking enough shots and owning enough of each one. It does not supply a universal number of companies.
