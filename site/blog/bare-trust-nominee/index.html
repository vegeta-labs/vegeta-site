Bare trusts: one name on the register, many owners behind it

How a UK nominee holding works, what the law treats you as owning, and the parts that are decided by the documents instead of by the label.

Updated 7 November 2026

When several investors back a UK company through a nominee, the company's register shows a single holder. That can look as though the investors own nothing the law would recognise. The reverse is closer to the truth: the nominee holds the name, and the investors hold the value.

Two kinds of ownership

A bare trust splits ownership in two. The trustee is the legal owner and appears on the record. The beneficiary, in HMRC's words, has the right to all of the capital and income. HMRC also notes that trustees of a bare trust are often called nominees, especially where the asset is shares in a limited company, so the two words tend to describe one arrangement.

Do not expect the company's records to show any of this. For companies registered in England and Wales or Northern Ireland, the Companies Act says no notice of any trust may be entered on the register of members. Your evidence of ownership is the trust documentation and the nominee's own records, so keep your copies.

Say Bulma's syndicate holds 300,000 shares in Senzu Labs through a nominee. Goku is entitled to 120,000 of them and Piccolo to 90,000, and other investors hold the remaining 90,000. Senzu Labs' register has one line for all 300,000. The nominee's records hold the three-way split, and that split is what determines who gets paid.

Tax follows the beneficiary

For UK capital gains tax, property held by a nominee or bare trustee is treated as belonging to the person behind it, and the nominee's dealings are treated as that person's own. There is no separate gain inside the trust to tax first.

If Senzu Labs is sold at £4 a share, Goku's 120,000 shares produce £480,000 and Piccolo's 90,000 produce £360,000. Goku's gain is measured against what he paid, and he reports it himself. If he paid £150,000, his gain before costs is £330,000. It sits on his return, alongside any other gains and losses he has that year.

The same logic carries over to the UK's startup tax reliefs. Shares held for an individual by a nominee are treated as held by that individual for EIS purposes, and SEIS has a matching rule. HMRC is explicit that this does not stretch to investing through a partnership.

Leads should read one clause closely. The EIS and SEIS rules each have a separate provision for shares issued to a bare trust for two or more beneficiaries, which treats each beneficiary as having subscribed an equal slice of the total (EIS, SEIS). Syndicate stakes are rarely equal. So it matters whether each investor's shares are separately held for them or sit as one undivided block, and that should be settled before the shares are issued.

Registration

"Nominee" does not mean invisible to HMRC. The Trust Registration Service manual says there is no specific exclusion for bare trusts, and that a bare trust which is an express trust should generally register, although other exclusions may apply to particular arrangements. Non-taxable trusts generally have 90 days from creation, and GOV.UK warns that failing to register may bring a £5,000 penalty. Someone should own this task, and the deal checklist should name them.

Who can sell

A common belief is that a bare trust means a majority vote on exit. No rule of trust law says that. A voting threshold comes from the documents the investors signed, and different arrangements set different ones.

What the law does supply is the default position that the beneficiaries are in charge. The capital gains definition of absolute entitlement turns on the exclusive right to direct how the asset is dealt with. HMRC's trust manual says that competent beneficiaries can agree to break up a bare trust without the trustees' agreement.

The practical arrangement has to be built on top of that. A buyer wants one signature by a date, and twelve investors will not all answer in a week. So the documents usually say how instructions are collected, what happens with silence, and whether a majority can bind the rest. Two questions are worth raising with your lawyer when you draft those terms. If a clause takes the decision away from individual beneficiaries, does the arrangement still fit the absolute entitlement on which its tax treatment rests? And if someone other than the investors holds the power to decide, is that person managing assets belonging to another with discretion, a specified activity that generally needs FCA permission when done by way of business and the assets include securities?

Company-level terms sit above all of this. A drag-along in the shareholders' agreement can bind the nominee, whatever the investors would have preferred among themselves. Read the two sets of terms together.

What a bare trust does not do

It does not make the investment safer, since the value still depends on one company. It does not make your interest easy to sell, because a transfer needs a buyer and has to fit the company's rules. Nor does the label settle whether the arrangement is a collective investment scheme; that depends on the facts, including how much control participants really have and whether their contributions are pooled.

For the lead's file

Before closing, a lead should be able to produce four records: the signed trust terms for each investor, a schedule of each investor's holding that adds up to the nominee's line on the register, the decision process for a sale, and the trust's registration position. If you cannot reproduce that schedule from what is on file, fix it now. It will be much harder to rebuild when a buyer's lawyer asks for it.
