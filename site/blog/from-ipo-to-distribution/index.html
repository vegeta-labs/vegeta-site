From IPO to distribution: why a listing day is not a payout

Between a company listing and investors getting paid sit lock-ups, resale rules, sale decisions and the waterfall, and each can change the figure.

Updated 7 November 2026

The news says Senzu Labs has listed at £30 a share. Bulma's SPV holds 200,000 shares, so its members start doing sums: £6 million, on the £1.5 million they put in. Then they email to ask when the money is arriving.

The honest answer is months away at the earliest, and probably a smaller number.

The number members will actually receive

Start from the end. Suppose the SPV sells half its holding soon after it becomes free to sell and the rest a little later, both below the listing price. Assume £50,000 of costs and 20% carry on profit, with no preferred return.

Item Amount
100,000 shares sold at £22 £2,200,000
100,000 shares sold at £20 £2,000,000
Gross proceeds £4,200,000
Costs −£50,000
Available to distribute £4,150,000
Return of contributions −£1,500,000
Profit £2,650,000
Carry at 20% −£530,000
Members receive £3,620,000

Members get £3.62 million, or about 2.41 times their money. The listing-day figure implied 4 times. Nothing went wrong: the shares simply traded down, and the headline left out the costs and carry.

Check the sum against the actual agreement. Reserves for tax or claims, staged distributions and member-level differences in fees or carry can all change who gets what. In a fund, other investments sit in the same waterfall, so a single exit may pay out quite differently depending on the fund's overall position.

Tell members this at the listing

Most of the frustration in these months comes from a gap in expectations, and clear reporting closes it. Keep four numbers apart in every update: shares held, value at the current price, cash realised from sales, and cash actually paid to members. Add the restrictions that apply and the next date something can change.

A line such as "200,000 shares held, locked up until 14 June, no proceeds yet" tells members more than a congratulatory note quoting the share price.

The dates you cannot sell before

First confirm what you hold. Before listing, the SPV probably held preferred shares, which usually convert under the company's charter into the class being listed. Check the ratio and the resulting share count against the registrar's or transfer agent's record, since a share split around the IPO can change it as well. Also check whether the SPV sold anything in the offering, because the prospectus names selling holders and what they keep.

Most early holders cannot sell on day one. In US IPOs, holders commonly sign a lock-up, typically for 180 days. That is a usual figure rather than a rule, so read your own agreement and the prospectus. Lock-ups can have staged releases or conditions linked to the share price or results date, and they can restrict moving shares to your own members, which matters for a vehicle planning to distribute shares.

The lock-up is contractual, and it is only one limit. For US-listed companies, a public resale of restricted securities relying on SEC Rule 144 has its own conditions, though a registered resale or another exemption can provide a different route. There is a minimum holding period of six months where the company has been a reporting company for at least 90 days, and one year otherwise. A holder who is an affiliate, broadly someone in a control relationship with the company, also faces a volume cap over any three months (the greater of 1% of the class or average weekly trading volume), manner-of-sale conditions, and a Form 144 notice above 5,000 shares or $50,000. If the vehicle's lead sits on the board, ask early whether it counts as an affiliate.

Other markets have their own versions, and the listing documents will set them out.

A free date is not clearance

A lapsed lock-up does not clear you to sell. Anyone with a board seat or a close line to management may hold information the market does not have. The SEC describes illegal insider trading as trading on material non-public information in breach of a duty, and tipping can count. For instruments on UK trading venues, the UK Market Abuse Regulation applies, with its own definition.

For a lead running an SPV, this has a practical side. Decide before the listing who receives company information, who decides on sales, and whether those should be the same person. Company insider policies can also impose blackout windows around results. Settle this while there is no pressure, not on the morning the lock-up lapses.

Cash or shares

The SPV's agreement says which options the lead has, and it needs reading closely. There are two main paths.

Selling and distributing cash keeps execution in one place. The lead picks the timing, members receive money and the sums are clean. But the lead carries responsibility for that timing, and a large block can move the price. Expiring lock-ups can themselves push prices down when many shares become sellable at once.

Distributing the shares in kind passes the decision to each member. Some will welcome this, for tax or conviction reasons. It requires each member to have a brokerage account that can receive the shares and a way to handle fractions. It also needs an agreed valuation for the waterfall, so everyone knows what price is used to calculate carry. Some members may be unable to hold the shares at all, and the agreement should say what happens to them. Check whether resale limits carry over to members after the distribution, too.

Either way, write down the policy and who can approve exceptions. A sale decision in a falling market is a poor moment to discover two people believe they hold that authority.
