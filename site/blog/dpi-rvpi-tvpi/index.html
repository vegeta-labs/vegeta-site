DPI, RVPI and TVPI: reading the numbers on a fund statement

Three ratios split a fund's reported return into money already received and value that is still only an estimate.

Updated 7 November 2026

Piccolo commits £500,000 to a venture fund. Six years later his statement shows £300,000 paid in, £90,000 distributed, and a £360,000 share of the fund's net asset value. Is he doing well? The statement can give him three ratios, and each answers a different question.

The three ratios

All three use the same denominator: what the investor has actually paid in. ILPA's reporting standards report each against paid-in capital, and its performance template divides net TVPI by capital contributed, so the £200,000 Piccolo has committed but not yet paid does not count.

Ratio Question it answers Piccolo's figures Result
DPI (distributions to paid-in) How much has come back? £90,000 ÷ £300,000 0.30x
RVPI (residual value to paid-in) How much is still in there, on paper? £360,000 ÷ £300,000 1.20x
TVPI (total value to paid-in) What is the total? £450,000 ÷ £300,000 1.50x

DPI plus RVPI equals TVPI, as long as they share a date and a basis. If they do not add up on a statement, ask why.

So Piccolo's 1.50x is mostly opinion for now: 0.30x is history and 1.20x is a valuation. That does not make the valuation wrong, but the two parts deserve different levels of trust.

What moves each number

Suppose a portfolio company raises a round at a higher price and the fund marks up its holding, adding £150,000 to Piccolo's share. RVPI rises to 1.70x and TVPI to 2.00x. DPI stays at 0.30x, and no money has moved.

If the markets turn and his share is written down to £150,000 instead, RVPI falls to 0.50x and TVPI to 0.80x. Again DPI is untouched. Once money has been distributed and cannot be called back, it stays counted.

That is why TVPI can fall and DPI cannot, unless more capital is paid in. A further capital call of £100,000 that goes straight into new investments at cost lowers DPI to 0.225x, and pulls RVPI and TVPI towards 1.0x, even though nothing good or bad has happened. Young funds calling capital show this effect all the time.

What counts as a distribution

A distribution is not always cash in the bank, and DPI does not always measure profit.

ILPA's performance template counts in-kind transactions, including stock distributions and withheld taxes, within permanent distributions. An investor who receives listed shares has a DPI number and also market risk until those shares are sold.

The same template separates recallable distributions, which return capital that can be called again, from permanent ones. If £30,000 of Piccolo's £90,000 were recallable, that money may need to go back in, so it pays to know how the fund treats it.

A DPI below 1.0x also means investors have not yet recovered what they paid, and even a DPI above it reflects capital returned as well as gain. It tells you nothing about how the money was raised. Proceeds of a sale and proceeds of borrowing against the portfolio look the same in the ratio.

Reading the residual value

Everything in RVPI rests on the valuation, so read the valuation notes. What is each major holding marked at, and on what basis? When was the last priced round? A headline company valuation is not necessarily what one class of shares is worth, because later investors may rank ahead on a sale.

Concentration matters as much as level. If one company makes up most of the residual value, the fund's outcome hangs on that one exit. Ask to see the largest holdings as a share of net asset value.

Same words, different basis

Before comparing two funds, check the basis. Net figures are after fees, expenses and carry, which is how ILPA defines net IRR, and its net TVPI is worked out for fee-paying investors. Gross figures are before them, and a gross deal multiple is a different measure again. Carry that would be due if the portfolio were sold at its marks may or may not have been accrued against the residual value.

Age matters too. A three-year-old fund with a 0.1x DPI and a twelve-year-old fund with the same DPI are in very different positions. Compare funds of similar vintage and strategy, and treat DPI in the early years as a weak signal.

Subscription credit lines add another wrinkle. A fund that borrows before calling capital changes the timing of its cash flows, so ILPA asks for IRR and multiple figures both with and without the facility.

Multiples ignore time

A 2x return over four years is about 18.9% a year. Over nine years it is about 8.0%. All three ratios above would look the same in both cases. IRR captures timing, and it needs reading together with the multiples because a high early IRR can sit on very little money returned.

The same ratios on a single-deal SPV

The arithmetic works for one company. Goku pays £50,000 into an SPV holding Senzu Labs. A partial secondary sale returns £20,000 to him after costs, and his remaining interest is valued at £90,000. His DPI is 0.40x, his RVPI 1.80x, and his TVPI 2.20x.

The questions are simpler, and the risk of relying on the residual is sharper. There is one company and one valuation, with no other holdings to balance it, so his TVPI depends on the current estimated value of one holding. His 0.40x, though, is already in his account.

An investor reading any statement should come away able to say how much has come back, how much is an estimate, who made that estimate, and how long it has taken to get there.
