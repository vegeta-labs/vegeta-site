UK rules

UK national security screening and the SPV

An SPV can trigger a mandatory filing under the UK's National Security and Investment Act, and putting a nominee or a holding company in the middle does not settle the question.

Updated 7 November 2026

Most SPVs take stakes far too small to attract a government filing. Some do not. A lead investor taking a large position in a UK company working on AI, robotics, satellites or engineered biology should test the deal against the National Security and Investment Act 2021 before fixing a closing date, because a miss can undo the deal itself.

Two questions decide whether a filing is mandatory

The first is about the company. Mandatory notification applies to entities carrying on specified activities in 17 areas of the economy, including advanced materials, artificial intelligence, defence, quantum technologies and synthetic biology. The sector names are broad but the definitions are narrow, so the test is what the company actually does, set against the detailed wording. A company formed abroad can be caught too, but only if it carries on the specified activity in the UK.

The second is about the size of the holding. Under section 8, control is gained when a person's shares or votes rise from 25% or less to more than 25%, from 50% or less to more than 50%, or from below 75% to 75% or more, or when the voting rights acquired let them secure or block any class of resolution. Those percentages are measured on nominal value and voting rights, so a cap table showing economic ownership may give a different answer.

A fourth route, material influence over a company's policy, is not subject to mandatory notification. It still matters, because the government can call in such a deal, and the parties can notify voluntarily. A board seat with a smaller stake is the kind of fact to raise with counsel.

Where the SPV changes the analysis

Suppose Senzu Labs does work that falls within the synthetic biology definition, and Bulma is putting a syndicate together. The structure produces different results.

First, a company SPV takes 30%. The SPV crosses 25% and is the acquirer. It must give notice before it gains control. The check does not stop at the SPV, however. Schedule 1 treats interests held indirectly through an unbroken chain of majority stakes as held by the person at the top. If Goku holds most of the votes in the SPV, the shares are treated as his as well, and his position needs its own analysis.

Second, a nominee holds 30% for twenty investors. Schedule 1 treats an interest held by a nominee as held by the person the nominee acts for, not by the nominee. With twenty investors of similar size, each holds about 1.5%, and on those numbers alone nobody crosses a threshold.

Third, the same nominee, but Bulma directs the votes. Schedule 1 also treats a person as holding a right they control, including a right exercisable in accordance with their directions. If the documents have the nominee vote as the lead instructs, the 30% of votes may be treated as Bulma's, however little money she has in the deal.

Fourth, an 18% SPV sits alongside Piccolo's direct 10%. Separately, neither reaches 25%. If they have agreed to exercise all or substantially all of their rights jointly in a way that is pre-determined by the arrangement, Schedule 1 treats each as holding the combined 28%. Voting agreements in a shareholders' agreement or a side letter deserve a read with this in mind.

Two further points catch people out. Each link in a chain can be a separate qualifying acquisition, and an internal reorganisation can count even when the ultimate owner stays the same, so moving a holding into a new SPV is not automatically safe. And a right that can only be exercised in certain circumstances, such as some conversion rights, can give rise to an acquisition when those circumstances arise or come under the holder's control. A note that converts past 25% should be looked at when it is issued as well as when it converts.

What happens if a filing is missed

Section 13 is blunt: a notifiable acquisition completed without approval is void. For an SPV, that puts the legal basis of the whole holding in doubt, along with everything that was built on it. Retrospective validation exists, but it is an application from a weak position.

The government's guidance also lists civil penalties of up to 5% of global turnover or £10m, whichever is greater, and possible criminal liability.

Falling outside the mandatory regime does not end the exposure. The government can assess a qualifying acquisition up to five years after completion, and within six months of becoming aware of it.

Build it into the timetable

The review period is up to 30 working days from the day the notification is accepted. If the deal is called in, there is a further assessment period of 30 working days, which can be extended by 45 more, and beyond that by agreement. For a syndicate with a hard closing date, a late discovery can be fatal to the round, and investors' money may be sitting uninvested in the meantime.

The practical sequence is short:

  1. Early in diligence, ask the company which of the 17 definitions it has considered and why it reached its view.
  2. Draw the real ownership chain on one page, with percentages of shares and votes at each level, and mark who directs each vote.
  3. Include convertible instruments, voting agreements and any existing holdings of the same investors.
  4. Where a threshold could be crossed in a sensitive area, have a lawyer who knows the regime read the chart against the definitions, and make clearance a condition of closing if a filing is needed.

Check the list on the day. In March 2026 the government announced that it intends to change the sectors through secondary legislation, bringing water companies in, giving semiconductors and critical minerals their own categories and taking off-the-shelf AI systems out of mandatory notification. The current rules apply until that legislation takes effect, so a company that was out of scope last year may not be next year, and the reverse is also possible.
