Leads and GPs

When a portfolio company asks the SPV for more money

A bridge request is a new investment decision with its own arithmetic. An SPV that has already spent its money also has a practical problem to solve before it can say yes.

Updated 7 November 2026

Two years after Bulma's SPV bought 10% of Senzu Labs, the founder calls. Customers are taking longer to sign, the next round has slipped, and the company wants £800,000 in a bridge at a £7.2m pre-money valuation. Existing investors are being asked to help.

Three pieces of work come before any answer: check the cash, work out what the new money achieves, and find out whether the SPV can act at all.

How long the cash really lasts

Ask for the bank balance and three months of actual receipts and payments. Leave out anything invoiced or signed but not yet received.

Senzu has £400,000 in the bank and is burning £120,000 a month, which gives about three and a third months. Management proposes cuts that bring burn down to £80,000. The redundancies cost £90,000 now, and the savings start in month three, once notice periods have run.

Scenario Cash after one-off cost First two months at £120k Left at £80k a month Total runway
Cuts, no bridge £310,000 £240,000 £70,000 about 2.9 months
Cuts, half the bridge (£400k) £710,000 £240,000 £470,000 about 7.9 months
Cuts, full bridge (£800k) £1,110,000 £240,000 £870,000 about 12.9 months

The first row is worth a pause: without new money the cuts make the runway shorter, not longer, because the savings arrive after the cost. The second shows why a minimum raise matters. A company that closes with half the money has under eight months, and a new funding round can easily take six of them. Commitments that lapse unless the round reaches a set total are a reasonable thing to ask for.

Then ask what the runway is for. "Thirteen months" is only useful if something happens within it that would make a new investor willing to pay: a named renewal, a pilot converted to a contract, a month at break-even. Without that, the bridge buys time and nothing else.

Find out what actually changed

Founders will often put the problem down to the market, and the market may well have moved. Higher rates and tighter credit can reach a small company through customers' budgets, through investors demanding more return for the same outcome, and through a thinner pool of later-stage money. Each of those shows up somewhere specific, so ask to see it.

If usage and renewals are steady and only new sales have slowed, the original case may be intact and the timing wrong. If existing customers are cutting seats or paying late, the product itself is in question, and more time may not help.

Weigh who is backing the bridge. A holder defending its earlier stake is a weaker signal than an outsider paying the same price. When the founder lists commitments, ask which one has no conditions attached. Sometimes none is.

Value the new cheque on its own

The post-money valuation is £8m, so the bridge buys 10% of the company. If the SPV takes up its share, that is £80,000 for 1%, which takes the holding from 9% back up to 10%. If it stays out, it is diluted to 9% and has spent nothing.

Suppose your honest view is a one-in-five chance of a £30m sale and nothing otherwise, ignoring later dilution and preferences. The £80,000 buys 1% of £30m, or £300,000, with a 20% probability: an expected £60,000. On its own, that is a poor bet.

The usual reply is that the money protects the existing 9%, worth an expected £540,000 on the same assumptions (9% of £30m is £2.7m, at 20%). That argument holds only if the SPV's cheque decides whether the bridge happens. If the round closes without it, the 9% is protected anyway, and the extra £80,000 has to justify itself as £60,000 of expected value. If the round fails without it, the protection is real and belongs in the sum. So the first question for the founder is who else is in, and how firmly.

The probabilities are judgement, and investors will differ. The structure of the question does not change.

Check where the instrument ranks. Many bridges are loan notes rather than shares, and that makes the holder a creditor until conversion. In a voluntary winding up in England, Wales or Scotland, section 107 of the Insolvency Act 1986 applies the company's property to its liabilities first, and only the remainder goes to members. If the company is close to that point, take insolvency advice before relying on any figure for equity value.

Can the SPV do anything?

An SPV normally holds a single position and has little cash beyond an expense reserve. It cannot follow on just because its investors would like to. Start with three questions.

Does it have the right? In a UK company, section 561 of the Companies Act 2006 gives ordinary shareholders a default right of first offer on new equity securities in proportion to their holdings, but a private company's articles can exclude it. Read the articles and the shareholders' agreement. Check any notice period too, because a bridge moves fast and a pre-emption window that closes while the SPV is still polling investors is a right in name only.

Can its documents take new money? Some allow further subscriptions, many do not. If the same vehicle takes the money, investors who decline may be diluted within it, and that needs a fair basis and a clear record. A second vehicle keeps the two positions separate, but it means new paperwork, new costs and a cheque that may be too small to carry them. On £80,000, set-up and running costs can consume a large share of the amount invested.

Who decides? The lead may have authority over the existing holding without having any over new money. Each investor in the original SPV should be told the terms, the alternative and the effect of saying no, then given a date. Bulma should also disclose any interest she has elsewhere in the round, such as a personal holding or a position in another vehicle taking part.

Keep the valuation question separate

Whatever the decision, the SPV's holding still has to be reported to investors. The bridge price is evidence for that figure, and not the whole of it: the 2025 IPEV Valuation Guidelines use the price of a recent investment to calibrate a valuation, say it is not automatically fair value, and require fair value to be estimated afresh at each reporting date. A bridge priced under pressure may not be representative. Record the valuation and the funding decision separately, each with its own evidence, and set a date to look at both again.
