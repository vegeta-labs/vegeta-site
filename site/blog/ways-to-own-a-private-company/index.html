For investors

Seven ways to hold a private company, and whose name is on the register

The route you take decides what you own, who votes, who can sell and what it costs, even when the company underneath is the same.

Updated 7 November 2026

"I've invested in Senzu Labs" can mean seven different things. It could mean Goku's name is in the company's register of shareholders. It could mean he owns a share in a vehicle that owns the shares, or a slice of a fund that holds Senzu among thirty others, or a listed share with a distant claim on all of them.

Ask the same four questions of every route. Who is the legal holder? Who decides on a vote or a sale? What sits between me and the proceeds? How could I get out?

The routes side by side

Route Legal holder Who decides Main thing to check
Direct subscription for new shares You You, within the share rights Share class and investor rights
Nominee The nominee, for you Depends on the nominee terms Whether votes and information reach you
SPV or syndicate vehicle The vehicle The manager or lead, under its terms Fees, carry, who can authorise a sale
Crowdfunding platform You or a platform nominee Often limited for small holders Share class compared with the lead investors'
Buying existing shares You, if the transfer is accepted You Seller's title, transfer consents
Private fund The fund The manager Commitment size, full fee stack
Listed investment company You own its shares; it holds the portfolio Its board and manager Share price against asset value

The first five put you close to a named company. The last two buy a manager's selection, and you may not choose Senzu at all.

What a layer costs

Take Goku's £20,000 and a round priced at £4 million post-money.

Direct, the arithmetic is £20,000 ÷ £4,000,000 = 0.5%.

Through Bulma's SPV, assume £1,000 of his money goes on set-up and running costs, and the vehicle takes 20% carry on profit. Now £19,000 reaches the company and buys 0.475%.

Run a £40 million sale through both, leaving out dilution, preferences and tax so that only the layer changes:

Direct Through the SPV
Stake 0.5% 0.475%
Share of sale £200,000 £190,000
Carry: 20% of profit over the £20,000 paid in none £34,000
Goku receives £200,000 £156,000

That gap is the price of the things an SPV can provide: a deal he might not otherwise reach, a lead who did the work, and one line on the company's cap table instead of many. Sometimes that is a good trade. Either way, it should be a known number before he signs, and the same sum applies at each layer of a feeder or fund of funds.

Being allowed in

Eligibility depends on the offer as well as on you. A few examples show how much this varies.

In the US, most private placements use Rule 506 of Regulation D. Under 506(b) an issuer can sell to unlimited accredited investors and no more than 35 non-accredited ones in any 90-day period. Regulation Crowdfunding is a separate route open to non-accredited investors, with limits set by income and net worth.

In the UK, the Financial Promotion Order lets certain promotions reach people who have signed a high net worth or sophisticated investor statement. That statement governs what can be sent to you. It does not make a given deal right for you.

Selling before an exit

Private shares are hard to sell. The SEC's bulletin on private placements warns that you may have to hold indefinitely, because you may struggle to find a buyer even when you are free to resell. Inside a vehicle, you may also need the lead's agreement, and your buyer may need to clear the same checks you did.

Buying from an existing holder has its own trap: you must confirm the seller owns what is on offer and can transfer it. Company agreements can give others a right of first refusal, and ordinary shares sold second-hand rarely carry the rights of the preferred shares in the last round. For UK investors, there is also tax to consider. EIS, SEIS and VCT income tax relief needs newly issued shares paid for in cash, so a purchase from an existing shareholder does not meet that condition.

Listed vehicles: liquidity with a different risk

A listed investment company is the easy route for most people: open a dealing account and buy. Yet the share price and the portfolio's value are two separate figures. The Association of Investment Companies shows the sum: net assets of 100p with a 90p share price is a 10% discount. If that discount widens while you hold, your return falls short of the portfolio's.

Buying shares in a listed private equity manager is something else again. You then own part of the business earning the fees, not part of the funds it runs.

Private equity is not one strategy

Venture investing sits under the wider private equity label, but the risk differs from a buyout. The SEC describes a typical private equity fund as taking a controlling interest in an operating business, with others making minority investments in fast-growing companies or startups. A buyout fund usually owns an established company and can often replace its management; a seed investor owns a minority stake in a young one and can mostly watch. Do not let a fund's label stand in for checking which of these you are getting.

Before you pick

Write one line for each route you are considering: legal holder, decision-maker, cost in pounds under a stated sale, and the way out. Then keep the signed documents and proof of ownership together, because in a vehicle those papers may be the only link between you and the company's register.
