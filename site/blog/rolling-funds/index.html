Rolling funds: a subscription that adds exposure, not liquidity

A rolling fund takes money each quarter, but what you own and when you can leave work very differently from a monthly subscription.

Updated 7 November 2026

A rolling fund replaces one large fund with a chain of small ones: a series of quarterly funds, sold as a continuing subscription. Investors take part in the investments made by the quarterly funds they join. Managers can keep admitting new investors as they invest.

You own a set of quarters

The programme's website may show every company it has ever backed. Piccolo's portfolio is smaller than that. If he joins in the third quarter and stays for two quarters, he has exposure to what those two quarterly funds invest in. The companies bought in the first quarter are not his, even if they are the reason he signed up.

So a performance figure for the whole programme says little about any one investor's position. An investor should keep a simple record for each quarter: how much he paid, what that quarterly fund holds, and what it has returned.

Stopping is not leaving

The subscription wording suggests something you can cancel. What you can stop is future payments. Money already invested stays in illiquid startups until they are sold or fail, which can take many years. An investor who cancels after four quarters still holds four quarters of positions.

Before subscribing, find the minimum subscription period, the notice needed to change or end it, and what happens to any money not yet invested. Ask too what tax paperwork to expect. US programmes built from partnerships usually issue Schedule K-1s, and a subscription spanning several quarterly funds may mean more than one.

How the carry can change the outcome

Whether the quarters are netted against one another can change an investor's result substantially. If carry is calculated across an investor's subscription period, the length of the period you sign up for affects when carry starts.

Piccolo puts $25,000 into each of four quarters, $100,000 in total. The first of his quarterly funds returns $90,000, the second returns $10,000, and the last two return nothing. Carry is 20%, and fees are ignored here.

If all four quarters are treated as one subscription, he got back $100,000 on $100,000. There is no profit and no carry, so he receives $100,000.

If each quarter stands alone, the first made a profit of $65,000, and carry on that is $13,000. The first quarter pays him $77,000. The second pays $10,000, a loss that does nothing to offset the carry. He receives $87,000 in total and has paid carry on a position that made no money overall.

Same investments, same money in, and a $13,000 difference. Two investors in one programme with different subscription lengths can legitimately end up with different results, so check which calculation applies to you before you pick a subscription length.

Marketing is allowed, but not to everyone

A US rolling fund that markets itself publicly needs an exemption that allows it, usually SEC Rule 506(c). That rule lets a manager talk about the raise publicly, but every purchaser must be accredited and the issuer must take reasonable steps to verify it. A public post about a fund is not an offer open to anyone who reads it.

Verification has become simpler for larger cheques. In March 2025 SEC staff accepted a high minimum investment as a route to verification, given written representations of accredited status and that the investment is not third-party financed, and no contrary knowledge. This is a staff position, not a rule. The request set those minimums at $200,000 for individuals and generally $1m for entities. Quarterly subscriptions below that level will need another verification method.

US permission to advertise says nothing about other countries. A UK-based manager, or one approaching UK investors, also has to deal with the UK restriction on financial promotions, which reaches communications from abroad that are capable of having an effect in the UK.

What it asks of a manager

The format needs steady deal flow. A thin quarter leaves its investors with money undeployed or concentrated in very few positions.

The income is also less secure than it first looks. Commitments are short compared with a ten-year fund, so a manager's revenue depends on investors renewing, while the research and reporting costs continue whether they renew or not.

Follow-ons need a written policy. When a company from an early quarter raises again, which vehicle may invest? An early-quarter investor's stake shrinks unless that quarterly fund has money left, while newcomers buy in at the new price. The same problem arises if the manager also runs SPVs or a conventional fund. Allocation rules should be written before the first such round, because at the moment of the round, each option favours somebody.
