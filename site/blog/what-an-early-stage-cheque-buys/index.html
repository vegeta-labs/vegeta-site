What an early-stage cheque buys: a percentage, some months and one question

Pre-seed, seed and Series A are loose labels, so judge an early deal by your stake after dilution, the runway it pays for and what that runway will prove.

Updated 7 November 2026

Round names are shorthand with no agreed definition. A "pre-seed" can buy a prototype in one sector and paying customers in another. Three things are firmer than the label. Follow Bulma's SPV as it puts £300,000 into Senzu Labs.

First: the percentage you end up with

Senzu raises £1.5 million at a £7.5 million post-money valuation. The new investors own 20%, and the SPV's £300,000 is 4%.

That 4% is a starting point. A company at this stage nearly always needs more money, and each new round issues new shares. Suppose Senzu goes on to do two more:

Event New shares sold SPV's stake
Seed 4.00%
Series A 20% of the enlarged company 3.20%
Series B 15% of the enlarged company 2.72%

Multiply through: 4% × 0.80 × 0.85 = 2.72%. A top-up to the option pool works the same way and comes out of everyone's share. Now test the outcome. On simple pro rata terms:

Sale price SPV receives Multiple of £300,000
£15m £408,000 1.36x
£80m £2,176,000 7.25x

Treat both figures as ceilings. Later investors usually hold preferred shares that come out first, so at a low price like £15 million the seed investors could get far less than £408,000, or nothing. And an SPV has its own costs and carry to take off before anyone sees cash.

Run this sum before investing, using the sale price you think is realistic rather than the one in the deck. It is also the honest answer to whether the entry price is reasonable: you are paying today for a share of a future outcome, after other people's money has been added.

If the instrument is not a share

Plenty of early rounds use convertibles rather than priced shares, and then no percentage is fixed until conversion. Y Combinator's post-money SAFE makes the sum simple at the cap: investment divided by post-money cap. So $250,000 on a $5 million cap comes to 5%.

The trap is in stacking. Each post-money SAFE takes its own percentage out of the founders, so several together add up, and the next priced round then dilutes all of them. Convertible notes have their own interest and maturity terms. A cap is also a ceiling for conversion, not a valuation anyone has tested. YC publishes its forms and a user guide, written for specific jurisdictions; the US forms are for US-incorporated companies, and deals elsewhere need local equivalents.

Ask for the whole capitalisation: every outstanding share, option, note and SAFE. A percentage that ignores them is not a real percentage.

Second: the months the money pays for

Now turn the raise into time. Senzu has £1.5 million. Take off £100,000 for one-off costs, which leaves £1.4 million. At a net burn of £70,000 a month, that is 20 months.

Raising the next round usually takes months, and a company with little cash left negotiates badly. If Senzu wants six months of cover while it does so, it has to show its results by month 14. So the real plan has a 14-month budget, even though the round looks like it buys almost two years.

Stress it. If burn rises to £90,000 once the planned hires arrive, £1.4 million lasts about 15.5 months, and the same six-month margin moves the deadline to month 9 or 10. Small changes in hiring move the deadline far more than founders expect, so ask to see the plan with a slower sale cycle and later revenue.

Third: the question that money will answer

Every early round should buy an answer to something a later investor cares about. Vague goals like "grow revenue" or "extend the platform" do not count. A useful question has a clear yes or no: will three customers with no prior link to the founders pay full price and renew? Can the device be made at a cost that leaves a margin?

Then compare the answer's date with the runway. If the first renewal cannot happen until month 18 and the deadline is month 14, the round is too small for its question, whatever the terms.

It also tells you what stage a company is really at. Ignore the label and list what is known: does the product work, do people pay, do they stay, can the company serve them at a profit, and does any of it still rely on a founder doing it by hand? The further down that list a company has got, the less risk is left for you to take, and the more you should expect to pay.

What is different in an SPV

An SPV usually writes one cheque. When Senzu raises again, there is often no money in the vehicle to follow on, so any further investment means a fresh raise from the members, on new terms, with fresh decisions. Say so to investors at the start. They should expect the diluted figure, not the 4%, unless they choose to invest more.

Treat any follow-on as a new investment. The evidence may have improved while the price rose faster. The right comparison is the new round against other uses of the same money, not against the wish to protect the original stake.

When a new round comes, the three checks still apply: what percentage this buys after later dilution, how many months it pays for, and what it will prove by the time those months run out.
