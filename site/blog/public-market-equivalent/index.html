Did it beat the market? How a public market equivalent works

A multiple shows that an investment made money. A public market equivalent shows whether the same cash, on the same dates, would have done better in an index.

Updated 7 November 2026

An investor looking at a 1.4x multiple knows the investment gained value. What the multiple cannot say is whether that was a good result for the risk and the wait. Comparing a fund's IRR with an index's annual return does not settle this either, because a fund draws and returns money at irregular times, while an index return assumes one lump sum invested throughout. The public market equivalent, or PME, handles this by running the fund's actual cash flows through the index.

The Kaplan-Schoar calculation

The best-known version comes from Steven Kaplan and Antoinette Schoar's study of private equity returns. They compare the money coming out of a fund with the money going in, after adjusting each flow by a total return on the S&P 500. A result above 1 means the fund beat the index on that measure. Below 1, it lagged.

To calculate it yourself, take each cash flow and grow it to the measurement date using the index: multiply by the closing index level, then divide by the level on the day of the flow. Add up the grown distributions plus the remaining value, and divide by the grown contributions. Remaining value is measured at the end date already, so it stays as it is.

An example with uneven cash flows

Goku puts £100,000 into an investment at the start and another £50,000 two years in. After five years he receives £120,000, and after six his remaining stake is valued at £90,000. The chosen total-return index stands at 100 at the start, 120 after two years, 150 after five and 160 after six.

Flow Amount Index that day Grown to year 6
Contribution, start £100,000 100 £160,000
Contribution, year 2 £50,000 120 £66,667
Total contributions £150,000 £226,667
Distribution, year 5 £120,000 150 £128,000
Remaining value, year 6 £90,000 160 £90,000
Total out £210,000 £218,000

The PME is £218,000 ÷ £226,667, or 0.96.

His multiple is 1.40x and his IRR is about 7.3%. On their own, both look like a respectable result. Yet the index was growing faster, and the same cheques on the same dates would have ended slightly ahead. The £90,000 is also only a valuation, so the final figure could still move.

The method works for one deal

Nothing in the arithmetic needs a fund. An SPV with one contribution and one distribution reduces to a single line. If Piccolo pays in £80,000 and receives £200,000 seven years later, while the index doubles, the PME is £200,000 ÷ £160,000, or 1.25.

A lead showing several past SPVs can run the calculation across all of them together, as long as all the deals go in, the winners and the write-offs, each at its real dates.

Choosing the benchmark changes the answer

Cambridge Associates publishes a modified version of PME, called mPME, that buys and sells index shares according to the fund's cash flow schedule, then expresses the outcome as a return. Its benchmark book for 31 March 2026 sets one US venture index, net of fees, expenses and carry, against several public indices at once.

Period to 31 March 2026 US venture index Russell 2000 mPME Nasdaq Composite mPME
1 year 26.13% 25.41% 25.40%
5 years 7.43% 4.84% 11.57%
10 years 15.99% 9.85% 17.22%

Over ten years, the same venture returns beat one index by more than six percentage points and trailed the other by more than one. Neither comparison is wrong. They ask different questions: did venture beat smaller listed companies, or did it beat a technology-heavy market? The figures also show how the period matters, since the one-year result looks very different from the five-year one.

This also explains why a benchmark should be chosen before the results are known. A manager picking the index that flatters them is reporting the choice as much as the return.

Read the benchmark's construction too

That venture figure is capitalisation-weighted, and Cambridge notes that the largest vintage years mainly drive it. The figure is a pooled result for US funds, built from what managers report. It does not describe a typical fund, and it is not what any one investor could have bought.

The basics of the index matter as well. Use a total-return series with dividends included, or the benchmark is understated. Use the currency the investor's money actually moved in. A sterling investor in a US-dollar fund and a dollar investor in the same fund can have different results. Keep gross and net cash flows apart, and be sure the flows you use are the ones the investor actually experienced.

What PME does not measure

PME is a comparison of cash outcomes and not a full risk adjustment. Kaplan and Schoar themselves note that if private equity has a market beta above one, PME may overstate its risk-adjusted performance. Liquidity is missing too: an index holding can be sold today, and a venture stake usually cannot. A PME of exactly 1 therefore implies an investor took on illiquidity and concentration for no additional return.

It also inherits every weakness of the remaining value. Where much of the answer is an unrealised mark, the PME carries that uncertainty into its result, and it does so without showing it. The ratio is most reliable late in a fund's life.

Used this way, PME is a sharp tool for a narrow question: given what this money actually did, would a plain index have done better over the same dates?
