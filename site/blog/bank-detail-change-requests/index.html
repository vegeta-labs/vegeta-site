When an email asks you to change bank details

A request to pay a different account should never be settled from the inbox, and the UK rules that refund scam victims cover less of a syndicate's money than people assume.

Updated 27 November 2026

Explore

In 2025, criminals took £576.4 million through authorised push payment fraud in the UK, £75.6 million of it from businesses. "Authorised" means the victim sent the money themselves, deceived about who would receive it or what it was for. In a syndicate the trick arrives in two ways: someone posing as an investor asks the lead to pay a distribution to a new account, or someone posing as the lead tells investors the vehicle's collection account has changed.

The investor who has "moved banks"

Senzu Labs has been sold, and on Friday Bulma's vehicle pays out £240,000.

Investor Distribution
Goku £96,000
Piccolo £72,000
Krillin £40,000
Tien £32,000
Total £240,000

On Thursday afternoon an email arrives under Piccolo's name. He has moved banks, the new details are in the attached bank letter, he is travelling and can only be reached by email, and he needs confirmation today so that Friday's payment is not delayed.

The trick also runs the other way. When Bulma collects for her next deal, Goku, who has committed £120,000, receives a message under her name saying the vehicle's account has changed, with another investor's "proof of payment" attached to show the new account is already in use.

Four signals, none of them proof

  • A sender that is nearly right. The display name is Piccolo's, but the address differs by a character from the one on file, or replies go somewhere else. Scammers impersonate someone you correspond with regularly.
  • Changed details. Government guidance for businesses lists a message asking to change bank account details among those to treat with caution, alongside unusual or urgent payments.
  • Pressure, and a reason not to phone. A deadline today and "email only" both discourage the one check that would expose the request.
  • An attachment offered as proof. A bank letter or payment confirmation is the sender's own document. It shows nothing about who controls the account.

Absent signals prove nothing either. If Piccolo's real mailbox has been taken over, the address is correct and the message still is not his.

The call back, and what to write down

The same guidance says to verify the request by phoning a trusted contact, and not to reply to the email, because the address may be cloned or hacked. For a syndicate that means the phone number already on file from Piccolo's subscription, never a number in the email, its signature or the attached letter.

Bulma calls it. Piccolo has not moved banks, and Friday's £72,000 goes to the account on file.

Whatever the answer, a careful operator records:

  • the original email, kept with its headers, and when it arrived;
  • who called, when, on which number, and where that number came from;
  • who answered and what they confirmed;
  • a second person's approval, given after the call, by someone who did not make it;
  • a note sent to the old contact details saying a change was requested;
  • the decision, and when any new details were first used.

Investors need the same rule in reverse: tell them at the start that account details never change by email alone, and give them a number to call.

What the name check does and does not do

When a new payee is set up, the bank can run confirmation of payee, which checks the name, sort code and account number against the receiving account. The answer is a match, a close match, no match, or a message that the check was not possible. It covers domestic UK payments only.

A match says the account is in the name you typed. It does not say the account belongs to the person you meant to pay. If the fake email tells Goku that the new account is held in an administrator's name, and he types that name, a match confirms only what the fraudster told him. Pay.UK says the service is "not a silver bullet" and does not guarantee that a fraudulent payment will be detected.

What the reimbursement rules cover

Since 7 October 2024, banks and payment firms must reimburse victims of this kind of fraud. The Payment Systems Regulator's protections apply to individuals, microenterprises and charities (charities with an annual income below £1 million), for payments made by Faster Payments or CHAPS from one UK account to another. The most that can be claimed is £85,000, firms may deduct an excess of up to £100, though not from a vulnerable consumer, and a claim should be made within 13 months.

Apply that to the two emails.

Goku pays as an individual from a UK account, so the rules can apply to him. But if he sends £120,000 to the false account, at most £85,000 comes back under them, and at least £35,000 does not. The regulator notes that a larger loss can be taken to the Financial Ombudsman Service, which is a complaint, not an entitlement.

Bulma's vehicle is not an individual. It is covered only if it counts as a microenterprise, which the regulator's direction defines as fewer than ten persons employed and an annual turnover or balance sheet total of no more than €2 million. Whether a particular company, or a nominee holding money for others, meets that test is a question for its bank and a solicitor, and worth asking before any payment day.

The regulator's summary for payment firms lists exclusions, among them international payments, civil disputes and gross negligence, so an investor paying from an account abroad is outside the rules entirely. Gross negligence here means grossly negligent failure to meet the caution the rules expect of a consumer. The regulator calls it a high bar, it is for the firm to show, and it cannot be used against a vulnerable consumer. Claimants are also expected to have regard to their bank's warnings and to report promptly.

The regulator itself is changing. The government has decided to consolidate the PSR's functions within the FCA, and a Bill introduced in 2026 would carry that out. The reimbursement requirements would move with it, and the FCA would then set its own approach.

None of this is deposit protection, which covers a bank failing and nothing else.

If the money has already gone

The NCSC's advice is to contact your bank through its official website or phone number, not through details in the suspicious message. Then tell the real investor through the details on file, keep every message, and take legal advice on where the loss falls.
