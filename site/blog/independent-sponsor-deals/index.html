Investing with an independent sponsor: follow the fees and the carry

Independent sponsors raise equity one acquisition at a time, and the same deal can pay the sponsor three different ways.

Updated 7 November 2026

An independent sponsor, sometimes called a fundless sponsor, buys private businesses without a committed fund behind them. They find a target, get it under an exclusive letter of intent, then raise equity for that one acquisition from family offices, funds or individuals, and usually help oversee the company afterwards.

For investors, this differs from a fund commitment in an obvious way: you see the exact company, price and terms before you say yes. But you also have to judge the economics deal by deal. There is little standard wording, and a sponsor's pay reaches you through several routes at different levels of the structure.

What deals look like

A useful public dataset comes from a US law firm active in the area. McGuireWoods' deal study draws on nearly 300 survey responses about transactions closed from 2018 to 2021. It is a survey of one market in a particular period, so read it as a reference point and not as a current price list. More than 75% of the deals had a target enterprise value between $10m and $75m, and roughly two-thirds were bought at under six times EBITDA.

It divides sponsor pay into closing economics, a management fee and carried interest. Here is what each one looks like in a sample deal.

Where the money goes on day one

A sponsor agrees to buy a company with $4m of EBITDA for $22m, or 5.5 times, on a cash-free, debt-free basis, with no equity retained by the seller.

Uses $m Sources $m
Purchase price 22.00 Senior debt (3 times EBITDA) 12.00
Sponsor closing fee (2% of enterprise value) 0.44 Equity 12.00
Other deal costs 0.76
Cash left in the company 0.80
Total 24.00 Total 24.00

Look at how much the equity has to cover. The price less the debt is $10m, but the cheque is $12m. Only $0.80m of the $2m difference stays in the business. The other $1.20m is paid away in fees and costs on day one, before the business has done anything.

The 2% is not arbitrary. In the study, 2% of enterprise value was the most common closing figure for deals of $10m to $75m. Sponsors put more than half of their closing economics back into the deal in approximately 60% of transactions, most commonly all of it. That is worth asking about directly. A sponsor who takes out $440,000 and leaves nothing in is in a different position from one who reinvests the lot and stands beside you in the equity.

The fee that continues every year

After closing, the company usually pays the sponsor a management fee. In the study, 60% of deals paid 5% of trailing EBITDA, and most deals set a floor, a cap or both. Here that is $200,000 a year, or $1m over five years if earnings stay flat. It is a real cost to the business: every dollar of it would otherwise go on paying down debt, reinvestment or distributions.

There is a second thing to check. Lenders normally rank ahead of this fee and can block it when the company breaches its covenants. Of the deals with credit agreements, 86% let the fee keep accruing while blocked. So in a bad year, a debt to the sponsor can build up behind the scenes, payable when things improve. Ask whether fees accrue while blocked, where the accrued amount ranks against your distributions, whether it earns interest and whether it stops if the sponsor is removed.

Who gets what at exit

Carried interest is where documents diverge most. The study describes two models: a straight percentage of profit after capital is returned, often after a preferred return as well, and a tiered model where the sponsor's share rises as returns clear hurdles. The tiered model was used in 61% of deals, and in 70% of those, hurdles were set as a multiple of invested capital, not as an annual rate of return.

The difference shows up clearly when you run both across different outcomes. Take the $12m of equity, ignoring any preferred return. Option A pays the sponsor 20% of profit. Option B pays 10% of profit up to two times capital, 20% from two to three times and 30% above three. To keep the sums simple, the tiers here are measured on total equity proceeds. Many real agreements measure on what investors have received, which moves the breakpoints.

Equity proceeds at exit Multiple Sponsor, option A Investors, option A Sponsor, option B Investors, option B
$18m 1.5x $1.2m $16.8m $0.6m $17.4m
$30m 2.5x $3.6m $26.4m $2.4m $27.6m
$48m 4.0x $7.2m $40.8m $7.2m $40.8m

Here the tiered version is better for investors on every outcome below 4x, and the two match exactly at 4x. Above that, it pays the sponsor more. Investors pay less on mediocre results and more on strong ones. A sponsor who wants option B is betting on the upside. Numbers like these are the way to compare any two proposals, because percentages alone tell you very little.

Time matters too. Multiple-based hurdles pay the same whether a business doubles in three years or in nine, and investors in a slow deal have earned far less each year. The study found 17% of tiered deals using a mix of IRR and multiple tests, often for that reason.

Things to settle before you wire

Who pays if the deal dies? A sponsor can run up large legal and diligence bills before any investor is bound. Find out whether investors are being asked to share these costs.

How firm is the equity? If some of the money is only indicated interest, what happens if it fails to show up at closing? If you are committed, when does your money get called, and what are the conditions?

What can the sponsor do without you? Check who controls the board and whether investors must approve more debt, new equity, add-on acquisitions, payments to related parties and a sale of the company. Think also about removal: can the sponsor be replaced for poor performance, and what happens to fees and carry if so?

How does your money get to the company? Equity usually passes through a holding entity, and lenders may hold security at or above the operating company. You should be able to draw the chain from your subscription to the operating business, with each layer's costs, rights and restrictions.

Is the sponsor's pay itself a regulatory question? McGuireWoods notes that the structure of closing economics can raise regulatory and licensing issues. The SEC's guide to broker-dealer registration asks whether compensation depends on a transaction's outcome or size, and says a yes to any of its questions may mean registration is needed. Other countries apply their own rules. The sponsor should be able to tell you how they have addressed this, and who advised them.
