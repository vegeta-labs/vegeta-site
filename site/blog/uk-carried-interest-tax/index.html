UK rules

How the UK taxes carried interest since April 2026

UK carry now falls within income tax, and how much of it gets the lower effective rate depends on how long the investments were held.

Updated 7 November 2026

From the 2026-27 tax year, section 58 of the Finance Act 2026 treats an individual who receives carried interest for investment management services as carrying on a trade. The carry is taxed as trading profits, within income tax and Class 4 National Insurance. It replaces the capital gains treatment, which had already moved to a 32% rate from April 2025.

The new rules apply to services "whenever performed", so a carry deal signed years ago is caught if the carry arises now.

The 72.5% rule

Only 72.5% of qualifying carry counts as taxable profit, while non-qualifying carry counts in full. Using the 2026-27 rates, a top-rate taxpayer outside Scotland pays 45% income tax plus 2% Class 4, or 47%. That gives these top effective rates:

Taxpayer Rate on non-qualifying carry Rate on fully qualifying carry
Higher rate (40% + 2%) 42% 30.45%
Additional rate (45% + 2%) 47% 34.075%
Scottish top rate (48% + 2%) 50% 36.25%

The holding-period table

How much of the carry qualifies comes from Schedule A1, inserted by Schedule 11. It depends on the investment scheme's average holding period:

Average holding period Share of carry that qualifies
Under 36 months 0%
36 months, under 37 20%
37 months, under 38 40%
38 months, under 39 60%
39 months, under 40 80%
40 months or more 100%

The average is weighted by value. For each investment, multiply the value invested by the time it has been held, add the results, and divide by the total value invested. Each holding is measured up to its sale or, if it is still held, up to the moment the carry arises. A fund that held £60,000 for 60 months and £40,000 for 24 months averages 45.6 months, even though one holding fell well short.

Why a single-company SPV is exposed

A fund can absorb one quick exit. A single-company vehicle has nothing to average against, so its one holding period usually is the average.

Suppose Bulma is an additional-rate taxpayer entitled to £60,000 of carry from a Senzu Labs SPV, with no permitted deductions. The tax depends on when the carry arises:

Time held at sale or when carry arises Qualifying share Amount taxed Tax at 47%
24 months 0% £60,000 £28,200
37 months 40% £53,400 £25,098
39 months 80% £46,800 £21,996
40 months or more 100% £43,500 £20,445

An offer at month 24 therefore costs Bulma £7,755 more tax than the same exit at month 40. Her investors face no such difference, as the holding-period test does not affect their tax. So a lead weighing an early sale has a personal interest that her investors do not share, and it is worth raising with them openly.

The clock runs from when the investment was made. Under the Schedule, new shares count from the date of issue, so the date investors paid into the SPV is not the one that matters. Share issue dates and any part-sales need to be right in the records. Later top-ups and partial disposals have their own rules, and venture capital funds that meet set conditions can treat some later investments in a company as made at the first date.

Does it apply to your vehicle?

Two parts of the definition need checking.

First, the vehicle must be an "investment scheme", which means a collective investment scheme or an alternative investment fund, as section 23R defines them. The collective investment scheme test looks at whether participants lack day-to-day control, and whether contributions are pooled or the property is managed as a whole. The legal structure of an SPV matters here, so a lead should get this answered for their own vehicle before closing and not at exit.

Second, the individual must perform "investment management services". The term is broad: it includes advice, seeking funds from investors, researching potential investments, and acquiring, managing or selling assets for the scheme. A syndicate lead's ordinary work can easily meet it.

The sum is normally a profit-related return. Under the Schedule, that means it arises only if there are profits, varies to a substantial extent with those profits, and investors' returns turn on the same profits. There is also a second route: a sum paid from profits only after all or substantially all the capital is repaid and each external investor has a return equivalent to at least 6% a year, compounded annually, is treated as carried interest. Otherwise, any amount that was never at significant risk is not carried interest.

Details that catch people out

Selling the right to carry does not avoid the rules. Money received for disposing of it is itself treated as carried interest, unless it is taxed as a disguised fee.

A lead's own investment is treated separately. A return on a co-investment made on arm's-length terms is not carried interest.

Carry can arise before cash arrives. The rules cover loans, advances and allocations of profit, and an individual can elect to be taxed earlier as scheme profits arise. Where carry arises before the holding test is met, it may be treated as fully qualifying on a claim, if it is reasonable to assume the test will be met. It stops being treated that way if the assumption fails, and the shortfall then becomes taxable.

Carry already taxed as employment income falls outside the new charge. What someone paid for the entitlement can count as a permitted deduction.

Location matters too. The regime covers individuals who are UK resident or whose carry relates to services performed in the UK, so someone working partly here can be caught even if they live elsewhere. Section 23K apportions the trade by UK workdays. When a non-resident allocates qualifying profits, days before 30 October 2024 do not count, nor do days in a non-UK tax year (one in which they were non-resident with fewer than 60 UK workdays) or days before a run of three or more such years. Non-qualifying profits have only a narrower exception, for anticipated qualifying profits. A UK workday means more than three hours of this work in the UK, which makes a record of working days worth keeping from the start.

Other countries set their own rules, and a lead with investors or work abroad cannot assume these apply there.
