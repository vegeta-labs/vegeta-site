Side letters: keeping track of what one investor was promised

A side letter changes the terms for one investor, and it only works if the change reaches every calculation and every message that uses those terms.

Updated 27 November 2026

Explore

Bulma is raising £400,000 for Senzu Labs through a nominee SPV. The standard terms are a deal fee of 2% paid on top of each investment and carry of 20% of profit. Goku will put in £100,000, a quarter of the round, and asks for something in return. Bulma agrees to a letter: no deal fee for him and carry of 10%. Two other investors ask for smaller things. Nothing about the round looks different, but there are now three sets of terms to apply for the next seven years.

What a side letter is

A side letter is a separate agreement with one investor that adds to or changes the main documents for that investor. The subjects are usually money (fees, carry), information (copies of the company's reports), rights (a seat in a later round, a right to transfer to a family vehicle) and promises about other investors' terms.

Two questions come before any figures. First, who is giving the promise. A letter signed by Bulma personally cannot oblige the nominee to do anything, and a fee waiver only works if whoever collects the fee is bound by it. Second, how it fits the main documents. An entire-agreement or variation clause in the main documents can affect whether the side letter takes effect, so how the documents fit together has to be settled when they are drafted. Both points are for the lawyer drafting the documents. The operations job starts once the letter is signed.

One register, one line per promise

Keep the letters in one place and keep a register with a line for each promise, not for each letter. A letter can hold several promises, and they are used at different times.

Investor Letter dated Promise Used when Checked by
Goku 3 March No deal fee Closing Allocation
Goku 3 March Carry of 10% instead of 20% Every distribution Distribution
Krillin 5 March Copy of the company's quarterly report Each quarter Investor updates
Piccolo 5 March Terms no worse than anyone investing the same or less Each new letter The register itself

The "Used when" column is the one that matters. Goku's fee waiver is used once, in March. His carry rate is used at every distribution, possibly years after anyone who negotiated it has moved on. Krillin's promise needs a reminder every quarter. A register that is read only when a letter is signed misses three of these four.

What the letter changes in the figures

At closing, the standard 2% fee would have been £2,000 on Goku's £100,000. Under the letter he wires £100,000 and pays no fee, so £100,000 is invested for him. Every other investor pays 2% on top: Piccolo wires £51,000 for a £50,000 investment.

At exit the difference is larger. Suppose the SPV's shares are sold for three times what was paid, with no hurdle and no costs, to keep the sums simple.

Goku, with the letter Goku, if the letter is missed
Invested £100,000 £100,000
Proceeds before carry £300,000 £300,000
Profit £200,000 £200,000
Carry £20,000 (10%) £40,000 (20%)
Paid to Goku £280,000 £260,000

If the distribution is worked out from the standard terms, Goku is underpaid by £20,000 and the carry recipient is overpaid by the same amount. Nobody notices unless the person running the exit distribution checks each investor's carry rate against the register before the money goes out. Correcting it afterwards means asking someone to pay money back.

Promises about other investors

Piccolo's letter is a most favoured nation clause: if anyone investing the same as him or less gets better terms, he gets them too. Its wording decides how far it reaches. This one compares investors by amount, so Goku's letter does not trigger it, because Goku is investing twice as much.

Now suppose Tien, investing £30,000, is offered a deal fee of 1%. Tien invests less than Piccolo, so the clause applies, and Piccolo's fee falls from 2% to 1%: £500 instead of £1,000. If that happens after closing, Piccolo is owed £500. The clause may also have a carve-out, for example for the lead's own investment or for fee terms given to a strategic partner, and the carve-outs are part of the promise.

This is why the register needs a check before every new letter, not only after it. Each new promise has to be read against the clauses already given.

Where the rules take an interest

Some promises are not safe to make at all where the round is under EIS. For the shares to qualify, they must not carry certain preferential rights to dividends, any preferential right to the company's assets on a winding up, or any right to be redeemed. The arrangements for issuing the shares must not include arrangements whose main purpose, or one of them, is to protect investors against the risks of the investment, by a guarantee or otherwise. The company must also meet a risk-to-capital condition, judged on all the circumstances when the shares are issued. A letter that promises one investor their money back is the obvious danger. So is a promise to buy the shares back or arrange their sale, and so is a benefit the company itself gives one investor. Any side letter in an EIS round should go past the tax adviser before it is signed.

If the vehicle is run by a full-scope UK alternative investment fund manager, the FCA's rules require the manager to tell investors, before they invest, how it ensures fair treatment of investors and, whenever an investor obtains preferential treatment or a right to it, what that treatment is, what kind of investor gets it and, where relevant, their links with the fund or the manager. Outside those rules, what the other investors are told depends on the documents, including any confidentiality clause in the letter itself. It is worth deciding at the start whether the existence of side letters will be disclosed, since an investor who finds out at exit that someone else paid less carry tends to ask why nobody said.

When the letter stops mattering

A promise lasts as long as its wording says. Goku's carry rate lasts until the last distribution. Krillin's report promise may lapse if he transfers his shares. When a promise ends, mark the line closed with the date and the reason rather than deleting it, so that anyone checking an old distribution can see which terms applied at the time.

Goten, Vegeta's deal calculations module, holds each investor's own fee terms and carry rate beside the deal's standard ones, so a closing and an exit are worked out with the terms each investor actually has.
