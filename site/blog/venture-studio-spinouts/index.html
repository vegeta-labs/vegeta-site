Backing a venture studio spinout: what to check that a normal startup doesn't need

When a company is built by a studio, the questions are who owns what, who pays whom and who is really running the business.

Updated 7 November 2026

A venture studio is an organisation that starts companies itself, repeatedly. It has a standing team of people such as engineers, designers and talent specialists, who test ideas, stop the ones that fail and spin the survivors out as separate companies with their own leadership. The studio takes a large stake in return. People also call this a startup studio or company builder.

The difference from an accelerator or fund is timing. The others meet a company that already exists. A studio is there before there is a company, which means a spinout's history is tangled up with its creator in ways a normal cap table doesn't show. If you are leading a syndicate into one, that tangle is your diligence list.

Run the cap table two rounds forward

Studio stakes vary, and there is no standard figure. They can be well above what an accelerator or angel would hold, because the studio contributed the idea, the early work or the first capital. Suppose a spinout has this structure before its seed round, then sells 20% in each of its next two rounds, with no pool top-up:

Holder Today After seed After Series A
Founding team 55% 44% 35.2%
Studio 35% 28% 22.4%
Option pool 10% 8% 6.4%
Seed investors 20% 16%
Series A investors 20%

Founders at 35% after a Series A, split between several people, with a passive shareholder at 22%, may be fine, or it may leave a CEO with too little reason to stay for the long haul. Later investors will ask. Some may push for the studio to give up shares before they invest, so find out now whether that is a realistic conversation. An option pool increase at each round would thin everyone further.

The question for the studio's stake is what it actually paid: cash in, named people for a stated period, finished work. A dedicated engineering team for 12 months is worth a lot. A promise of introductions is worth much less. List it and put a rough price on it, as a founder should have done before agreeing.

Check that the company owns what it sells

This is the most important legal question for a spinout. Code, brand and data were often created before the company existed, by people working for the studio or for contractors, and may sit in the wrong place.

Don't assume that paying for work means owning it. Under section 11 of the UK's Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988, an employer is first owner of copyright in a work an employee makes in the course of employment, subject to any agreement otherwise. Otherwise, the author is the first owner, which is how a contractor can end up holding rights in code unless they have assigned them. Even in the employee case, the first owner may be the studio and not the spinout. Rules differ elsewhere, so check the law that applies to each contributor.

Trace the chain, then. Each creator should assign to the studio or the company, and the studio should assign to the company. If the studio keeps the asset and licenses it instead, read the licence closely: how long it lasts, what it costs, whether it survives a sale of the company and what happens if the studio closes or is itself sold.

Price the shared services

A spinout may still rely on the studio for finance, hiring, engineering or office space after it launches. That is a real benefit, but it is also a related-party arrangement. Ask whether the work is already covered by the studio's equity or billed on top, or whether it is both. Ask how the rate is set, whether the company can leave, and who on the company's side approves the invoices.

Then work out what the costs would be when the arrangement ends. A company whose finance and engineering are provided at a discount has a hidden step up in cost when it starts to hire for them. If your milestones and runway assume the studio's rates forever, your model is wrong. The cash your round brings in may also pay the studio. If a tenth of the raise is going back to a shareholder, your SPV's investors should know.

Find out who is really in charge

A founder hired to run an idea the studio already chose is in a different position from someone who brought their own. Neither is better. But you are backing somebody, so find out who they are. Ask when the CEO joined, whether they are full time, whether they left a job to do it, and who wrote the plan you are reading. Check for studio staff who are also directors or officers of several sister companies, and how much of their week this one actually gets.

Control deserves a direct read too. Look at board seats, consent rights and sale provisions. A 28% holder with a veto on a sale or a new round is a more powerful presence than the percentage suggests, and the studio's incentives may differ from yours: it runs a portfolio and may have its own funding timetable.

If the studio also runs a fund, ask how follow-on decisions get made. A studio-affiliated fund investing in the next round in its own studio's company, at a price it may have influenced, is a conflict that deserves a clear process. Don't count on its support in a bad year unless it is written down. The fact that a studio built the company doesn't commit its fund to anything.

Which entity takes your money

The word "studio" can cover several entities, each giving an investor something very different. Money put into the studio itself pays for staff and overheads and buys whatever rights that company grants, which may or may not include any share of the companies it creates. Money put into a fund run by the studio buys an interest in that fund, subject to its fees and carry. Money put into a spinout, directly or through an SPV, buys a stake in that one company.

Goku, hearing that an SPV is "investing with" a studio, should be able to find out which one his money reaches. A lead should be able to show it on one page, with the entity name on each document.

How to read a studio's record

Studios tend to present their winners. Ask for a list of every company started and every idea funded and then stopped, with dates, and capital used for each. Then separate what the studio's owners made from what outside investors made in the same companies, since the first group paid far less per share. A long list of launches says little: what you want to know is how much value reached anyone, after what cost.
