Operations

Where an SPV's money actually is

A bank balance, a ledger and a legal entitlement are three separate things. Most cash problems in a closing come from treating them as one.

Updated 7 November 2026

Three days before Senzu Labs closes, the bank shows £279,975 in the account for Bulma's vehicle. The target is £300,000. It would be natural to say the raise is £20,025 short and start chasing someone for that amount. But that number describes no real event.

A shortfall made of three separate problems

Compare the bank statement with the list of who owes what.

Investor Expected Received Difference
Goku £120,000 £119,975 −£25
Piccolo £90,000 £90,000 £0
Third investor £10,000 £20,000 +£10,000
Fourth investor £30,000 £0 −£30,000
Others £50,000 £50,000 £0
Total £300,000 £279,975 −£20,025

Goku's bank took a £25 charge out of his transfer. The third investor paid twice and is owed a refund of £10,000. The fourth has not paid at all and owes £30,000. Once the refund goes back, the account holds £269,975, and £30,025 is outstanding.

The total alone would have hidden every one of these. Worse, the duplicate payment would have made the raise look closer to complete than it was. A reconciliation has to work line by line, against named investors, and every difference needs an explanation before anyone sends money to the company.

The account and the ledger

A bank account records what the bank owes the account holder. A ledger records who, among the people behind that account, is entitled to what. The two have to agree, but neither can stand in for the other.

Several vehicles can share one bank account, and a vehicle with its own line in a ledger may have no account of its own. So when a provider says funds are "ring-fenced" or "segregated", ask what that means in practice. Whose name is on the account? Is it shared with other deals? How often is it compared with the ledger?

For firms under the FCA's client money rules, that discipline is written down. A firm must compare its own records with those of third parties holding client money as often as necessary and at least monthly (CASS 7.15.22R), and must find the reason for any discrepancy its internal reconciliations show (CASS 7.15.29R). These rules apply to firms within their scope, so do not assume they apply to your vehicle's account. Ask whether the money is client money under those rules at all. If it is not, ask what control takes their place.

Four questions for each stage

The answers change as the deal moves along, so ask them of each stage: when investors pay in, when the money waits for closing, when it goes out to the company, and when proceeds come back.

  • Whose name is on the account?
  • For whom is the money held at this moment?
  • Who can instruct a payment, and is a second person involved?
  • If the deal fails, who returns the money, and are any costs deducted first?

The person who approves an investment should not be able to send the money alone. Treat any change to payment details as suspect until it has been confirmed through a channel other than the message that announced it, since an inbox can be compromised or spoofed.

Investors have a related question of their own. An unspent balance is no promise of a refund: the right to pull out ends when the commitment becomes binding under the subscription terms, which may be well before the money moves.

What deposit protection covers

UK deposit protection is often assumed to cover far more than it does. The PRA's limit is £120,000 for each depositor's total eligible deposits at a bank. It rose from £85,000 on 1 December 2025. How it applies depends on how the account is held.

Where a bare trust holds the deposit, the FSCS protects each eligible beneficiary up to £120,000, but that amount is shared with their other money at the same bank, including any bank that shares its licence. Say Goku's £119,975 share sits in a bare-trust account at a bank that also holds £50,000 of his savings. His total there is £169,975, so £49,975 is unprotected if that bank fails.

A company's own account is a different case. The company is a single depositor, so £300,000 in its name faces one limit, not one per shareholder. Not every depositor qualifies either: the PRA's rules exclude deposits by collective investment undertakings and financial institutions, among others. A vehicle could fall on the wrong side of that line, and the FSCS generally confirms eligibility only when a firm fails.

Deposit protection is about the bank failing. It does not cover errors in the ledger, a payment to a fraudster, an operator's failure, or the investment losing value after the money has gone to the company. Outside the UK, a different scheme applies, or none.

After closing

The cash question does not end at closing. A vehicle might keep some money back for expenses, and later it has to receive sale proceeds and pay them out. The same line-by-line discipline applies: each deduction traced to a term in the documents, and each payment traced to a named investor and a verified account. Check the total paid out plus costs and carry against what came in. If they do not match, stop and find out why before anything else goes out.
