Follow-on reserves: what the next cheque has to earn

A follow-on should be judged at today's price, on what the new money will return, and not on how well the first cheque has done.

Updated 7 November 2026

A fund's first cheque into Senzu Labs was £500,000 at a £5m post-money valuation, buying 10%. Eighteen months later the company raises £4m at £16m pre-money, £20m post. The fund has a right to take its pro-rata share. Should it?

Reserves are the capital a fund holds back for moments like this. Asking what percentage to reserve starts at the wrong end. The percentage is the sum of many decisions like this one, so it helps to get one of them right first.

The blended multiple flatters the follow-on

If the fund does nothing, the round dilutes it from 10% to 8%. To stay at 10%, it pays 10% of the new £4m, so £400,000 buys another 2%.

Suppose the company later sells for £100m. To keep the arithmetic plain, assume ordinary shares, no further dilution and no preferences.

Cheque Cost Stake Proceeds Multiple
Original £500,000 8% £8m 16x
Follow-on £400,000 2% £2m 5x
Together £900,000 10% £10m 11.1x

The fund's report will show 11.1x on Senzu Labs. The new money earned 5x, however, and the first cheque earned its 16x regardless. The first 8% is already owned, and whether to follow on doesn't change it. Only the 5x belongs in this decision.

Compare it with the alternative

The £400,000 could instead go into a new company at a similar £5m post-money, buying 8% and diluting to, say, 6.4%. Here is a comparison using assumed odds. The follow-on company is better known and more likely to survive, and the exit sizes are the same for simplicity.

Outcome Follow-on in Senzu Labs New first cheque
Sells for £100m 20% chance, 5x 10% chance, 16x
Sells for £40m 40% chance, 2x 20% chance, 6.4x
Returns nothing 40% chance 70% chance
Expected multiple 1.8x 2.88x
Assumed years to exit 5 8
Annual rate on expected multiple about 12.5% about 14.1%

On the multiple, the new company looks far ahead. Once time is included, the gap narrows sharply, and the follow-on loses money less often. Nudge the survival odds or the holding period and the order can flip. What the table offers is a way to argue about those inputs openly. The percentages are judgements, and nobody can look them up.

The real advantage of a follow-on is information. The fund has watched the company for eighteen months and should know more than an outsider would about the team, its numbers and how customers behave. That edge is real only if it changes the decision. If a fund follows on in every company that raises, it is not using what it knows.

What the money is for

Some follow-ons are not about upside. A bridge to keep a struggling company alive can protect the value of the existing stake, or avoid a punitive term for investors who do not take part. These are legitimate reasons, but they call for honest modelling: set out what happens to the first cheque with and without the new money, and treat the difference as part of the return.

Be wary of one justification, that the new cheque lowers the average cost or helps recover the old one. The old money is gone whichever way the decision goes. Terms need the same care. A new round may carry rights that rank ahead of earlier shares, which changes what both cheques would receive in a modest sale.

Building the number from the bottom

With a view on how decisions get made, the reserve can be built up. For a fund making 20 first investments, an estimate might run:

Step Figure
Companies expected to raise again 10
Companies the fund would back 5
Average follow-on cheque £400,000
Reserve needed £2m

Against a £12m fund, that comes to about a sixth, and the percentage arrives last. Then stress it. Rounds tend to bunch, since a tough market delays several raises into the same period, and a reserve large enough in total can still come up short in a given quarter. Valuations can also rise, making each pro-rata cheque bigger than the plan allowed for. A concentration limit should say how much of the fund can sit in one company, and what happens if a winner's rights exceed that.

Holding money back also has a cost. Reserved capital does no work until it is used, and if it goes unused, the fund has fewer shares in fewer companies than it could have had. Decide in advance when unused reserves are released to new investments or left uncalled, and keep a record of the follow-ons the manager declined and the reasons. Those records show investors whether selection is really happening.

When there is no reserve

A single-company SPV normally has no spare money after closing. Any pro-rata right still has to be funded, and that means going back to investors or forming a new vehicle. So several things need to be clear from the start. Who holds the right, the SPV or the lead? Can investors in the first vehicle choose to take up their share? Would a new SPV carry new fees and carry on the follow-on? How much time would everyone have to respond, given that rounds rarely wait?

Investors who expect a follow-on opportunity should see these answers in the documents and not rely on a lead's intention. Interest from past investors is not capital until it has been committed, and the follow-on still has to pass the same test as any other cheque: what does this money earn, from today's price, against what else it could do?
